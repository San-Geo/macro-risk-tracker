**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone with money in pensions, insurance products, or ‘alternative’ funds — private credit has quietly spread into retirement and insurance portfolios.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *First cracks, slow shakeout*


**Why this level:** Defaults are rising from a low base and high-profile blow-ups have appeared, but lock-ups and lower leverage point to a grind rather than a sudden break. **Raises it:** A correlated AI/software default wave; redemption gates at large funds; a widening gap between private marks and public prices. **Lowers it:** Defaults plateauing near historical norms; transparent, timely markdowns; no insurer-channel contagion.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Private credit — non-bank direct lending to mid-sized companies — has grown to roughly \$1.5–\$2.5tn in assets and now sits largely outside the prudential regulatory perimeter. In May 2026 the Financial Stability Board warned that its complexity, leverage, valuation opacity and interconnection with banks and insurers could amplify stress. The European Central Bank and Bank of England have echoed the concern; the BoE is stress-testing the sector.

**\[Established\]** The first real cracks appeared in 2025 with the collapses of First Brands and Tricolor in US auto-parts and asset-based finance, which pushed the question of hidden risk in private markets into view. Morgan Stanley warned in early 2026 that direct-lending default rates could climb toward 8% — versus a 2–2.5% historical average — with pressure concentrated in software borrowers exposed to AI disruption, while adding the spike would be “significant but not systemic.” Use of payment-in-kind arrangements, where borrowers defer cash interest, has risen — a classic stress release valve that also masks strain.

**\[Established\]** The most under-watched channel is the private-equity-to-insurance linkage: insurers affiliated with PE firms hold large private-credit concentrations, and a small but growing private-credit CLO market (around 3% of assets) is an early step back toward securitisation. In November 2025 Fed Governor Lisa Cook listed private credit among the vulnerabilities she is watching, alongside elevated asset valuations and hedge-fund Treasury positions, while saying she did not yet see it as a threat to financial stability.

### Why it is under-covered

It is private by design. There are no daily prices, no public ratings on most borrowers, and net asset values are reported with a 60–90 day lag using model-based marks. Opacity is the product feature that makes the risk hard to see — and hard to report.

### The debate

**The warning case:** lower-quality, higher-leverage borrowers; valuations that lag reality; insurer balance sheets and retirement savings quietly exposed; a “zero-loss” marketing myth meeting its first downturn.

**The reassurance case (bank CEOs, Treasury):** fund-level leverage is modest (roughly 1–1.5x versus 30–40x in pre-2008 securitisation), capital is locked up for years rather than runnable, and the market is “still small enough” to avoid systemic threat.

**Both sides accept:** defaults are rising from low levels, valuation practices are opaque, and the sector is untested through a full, prolonged downturn.

### Stress test (the benign case)

The structural differences from 2008 are real: long lock-ups prevent depositor-style runs, leverage is far lower, and there is no comparable web of CDOs. The most probable adverse path is therefore not a sudden collapse but a slow shakeout — rising defaults, forced markdowns, restricted redemptions, tighter credit for mid-sized firms — painful for investors and borrowers but absorbable by the banking system, which the Fed’s 2025 stress test suggested could withstand even severe non-bank stress. The risk to that benign read is correlation: if an AI-driven revenue shock hits software borrowers all at once, diversification fails exactly when it is needed.

### Historical parallel

The unavoidable comparison is subprime — opaque, rapidly grown, model-valued credit — but the analogy is contested and imperfect given the leverage and liquidity differences. A closer fit may be the slow-burn savings-and-loan and leveraged-loan workouts: not a single detonation, but a multi-year grind of markdowns and restructurings that traps capital and tightens lending well after the headlines fade.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Big investment firms have built a roughly $2 trillion business lending directly to mid-sized companies — outside the banks, and outside daily market prices. Because there are no public prices, trouble stays hidden until it surfaces, and the first cracks (a couple of large collapses) have appeared. It’s more likely to be a slow grind of losses than a sudden crash — but a lot of it has crept into insurance and retirement money, so ordinary people are exposed without realizing it.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Check your exposure.** Ask whether your retirement or insurance products hold ‘private credit’ or ‘alternatives,’ or read the fund factsheet.

**Be skeptical of ‘high steady yield, no volatility.’** Smooth returns often just mean the price isn’t being updated — not that there’s no risk.

**Mind the lock-ups.** Many of these funds can trap your money for years and value their holdings on a lag; don’t chase the yield without understanding both.

**Diversify.** Don’t let one yield-chasing product become a large share of your savings.
:::

### What to watch

- The gap between private marks and public-market comparables (e.g. liquid leveraged-loan and BDC prices): a widening gap understates realised losses.

- Rising payment-in-kind usage and redemption restrictions (“gates”) at large funds: signs strain is being deferred rather than resolved.

- Default rates in software/tech-services direct lending specifically — the AI-exposed segment.

- Disclosures on PE-owned insurers’ private-credit concentration: the channel into retirement assets.
