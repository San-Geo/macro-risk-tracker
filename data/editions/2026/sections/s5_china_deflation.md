**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone who buys goods, works in manufacturing, or invests — China exporting cheap goods and deflation reshapes prices, jobs, and trade politics worldwide.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Entrenched and radiating outward*


**Why this level:** Persistent, multi-year deflation with an export surge that is straining trading partners; structural and slowly worsening rather than acute. **Raises it:** A managed yuan depreciation; an escalating tariff/anti-dumping cycle; a local-government or bank stress event. **Lowers it:** Genuine consumption stimulus; producer prices turning positive; a negotiated trade de-escalation.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** China has now run roughly ten consecutive quarters of deflationary pressure — the longest stretch since its market transition in the late 1970s. Producer prices have been negative for over 30 months; consumer inflation averaged about 0% in 2025. The cause is a structural demand deficiency: a multi-year property crash (a fifth year of contraction, with tens of millions of vacant homes) has crushed household wealth and confidence, while state-directed investment keeps adding industrial capacity.

**\[Established\]** With demand weak at home, that capacity is sold abroad: China posted a record trade surplus near \$1.2tn in 2025. The IMF’s Article IV assessment is explicit that high export volumes are creating adverse spillovers, and a US Congressional Research Service summary cites an estimate that the renminbi is undervalued by around 16%. Beijing set its 2026 growth target at 4.5–5%, the lowest in decades.

**\[Single-source\]** One account puts the local-government debt pile near \$18.9tn against land sales that once supplied 40% of local revenue; **\[Established\]** the broader point — a doubling of total debt to over 300% of GDP and a local-government financing overhang that forces banks to roll loans to “zombie” firms — is well supported and entrenches the overcapacity.

### Why it is under-covered (as a global story)

China’s deflation is covered — as a China story. What is under-framed is the transmission: a \$20tn economy quietly exporting disinflation and excess supply into global tradable goods reshapes prices, margins and politics in dozens of other countries, feeding tariff fights, squeezing emerging-market manufacturers, and complicating every other central bank’s job.

### The debate

**The catastrophist view:** a debt-deflation spiral that ends in a balance-sheet crisis or a sharp devaluation.

**The stagnation view:** an “L-shaped” grind — low growth, persistent deflation, no collapse — Japanification with Chinese characteristics.

**Beijing’s view:** risks are contained, and “new quality productive forces” (EVs, solar, robotics, AI) will replace property as the engine.

**All sides accept:** the deflation is real and persistent, the consumer-demand response has been modest relative to the problem, and the export surge is straining trading-partner relations.

### Stress test (the benign case)

China has confounded collapse calls for over a decade. It retains enormous policy room (rates, reserve requirements, special bonds), a closed capital account that limits flight, and genuine global leadership in EVs, solar and automation that could yet rebalance growth. Cheaper Chinese goods also lower input costs and inflation for importers — a benefit, not only a threat. The flaw in the benign case: every quarter of deferred demand-side reform deepens the debt-deflation loop and widens the external imbalance that provokes a protectionist backlash.

### Historical parallel

Japan after 1990 is the canonical comparison: a property and balance-sheet bust followed by decades of deflationary stagnation despite heavy investment. The cautionary twist is scale — Japan was a creditor exporting capital; China is a far larger share of global manufacturing exporting goods, so its domestic deflation radiates outward with greater force, much as the 1930s saw beggar-thy-neighbour dynamics when surplus capacity met weak demand.

::: box
**In plain terms**

China’s economy is stuck in a slump where prices keep falling. To cope, it’s producing and exporting huge volumes of cheap goods. For shoppers elsewhere that can mean lower prices; for factories and workers in other countries it means brutal competition, and it fuels tariffs and trade fights. It’s a China problem that quietly becomes everyone’s problem through trade.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**As a shopper, cheaper imports can help you** — but expect more ‘trade war’ headlines and tariffs that can raise some prices and rattle markets.

**If your job or industry competes with Chinese exports** (manufacturing, steel, solar, EVs), watch for pricing pressure and plan accordingly.

**For investors, diversify.** Broad emerging-market and commodity exposure is sensitive to China’s slowdown.

**Don’t assume low inflation is permanent.** Trade fights can flip cheap-goods disinflation into cost-push inflation quickly.
:::

### What to watch

- China’s PPI and the CNY exchange rate: a managed depreciation would intensify exported deflation and invite retaliation.

- Export volumes vs values, and tariff/anti-dumping actions by the EU, US and emerging markets — the political feedback loop.

- Any genuine consumption stimulus (social safety net, direct transfers) vs more supply-side investment — the signal of whether Beijing is treating the cause or the symptom.

- Local-government financing-vehicle restructurings and bank net interest margins.
