**WHO THIS HITS:** *Mainly people in developing countries (through austerity and lost services); for everyone else it’s a moral, migration, and geopolitical story — and a risk inside emerging-market funds.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *High human toll, lower systemic spillover*


**Why this level:** A genuine, ongoing crisis for billions, but fragmented across many small economies and walled off from rich-world markets, so the global-financial threat is moderate even as the human cost is high. **Raises it:** A strong-dollar surge or commodity shock tipping several countries at once; stalled restructurings. **Lowers it:** Dollar and real-yield easing; faster G20 Common Framework debt relief; commodity-price relief.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** As of March 2026, 75 of 119 low- and middle-income countries assessed by the IMF and rating agencies are in or at risk of debt distress. UN figures put annual developing-country debt servicing near \$1.4tn, and more than 3.4 billion people — close to half of humanity — live in countries that spend more on debt interest than on health or education. Interest already exceeds 10% of government revenue in 56 countries, and 20% in 17 of them, a threshold strongly associated with default.

**\[Established\]** Africa is the epicentre. Restructurings (Zambia, Ghana and others) have been slow and partial; leaders argue the credit-ratings system punishes reform. A recent report found Nigeria’s debt payments running near five times its combined health and education spending. The 2026 Middle East energy shock worsens the squeeze for fuel importers, and a faith-led “Turn Debt into Hope” Jubilee campaign has revived calls for relief.

### Why it is under-covered

The UN Secretary-General named the exact reason: because the crisis is concentrated in poor countries, it is “not judged to pose a systemic risk to the global financial system” — so markets, and therefore most financial media, look away. It is a humanitarian and geopolitical catastrophe that is invisible precisely because it does not threaten rich-world portfolios.

### The debate

**Creditors and the IMF argue:** much of the debt is sustainable after fiscal adjustment; lending and reform programmes are the path back to market access.

**Critics argue:** the IMF functions as “the world’s debt collector,” its prescribed measures (e.g. VAT increases) are regressive, and the architecture forces a choice between servicing creditors and funding development.

**Both sides accept:** servicing burdens are at multi-decade highs, borrowing costs for these states run several times those of the US or Germany, and the diversion from health, education and investment is real and growing.

### Stress test (why it may not blow up)

There is a coherent case that this stays a slow burn rather than a 1980s-style cascade: exposures are fragmented across many small economies and many creditors (now including China and private bondholders), so there is no single domino. Multilateral backstops, the G20 Common Framework, and case-by-case restructurings can keep most countries limping along. But “no cascade” is not “no crisis” — the human and developmental cost compounds quietly, and a synchronised dollar surge or commodity shock could still tip several at once.

### Historical parallel

The 1980s Latin American debt crisis — the “lost decade” — is the template: foreign-currency debt plus a strong dollar and high global rates produced a decade of austerity, lost growth and political upheaval, ultimately resolved only by the Brady Plan’s debt write-downs years later. The lesson is that delay is itself a policy with a body count, and resolution tends to come late and via principal reduction, not endless rollovers.

**In plain terms**

Dozens of poorer countries now spend more on debt interest than on health or education, squeezing billions of people. It’s a real, ongoing crisis — but because it’s concentrated in poor countries and doesn’t threaten rich-world markets, it gets ignored. It’s unlikely to trigger a global financial crisis, yet it’s a slow humanitarian disaster and a quiet driver of migration and instability.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**This one is mostly awareness and values,** not a portfolio move — but it’s worth understanding as a force behind global migration and politics.

**Watch the US dollar.** A stronger dollar quietly makes this worse for many countries at once — a useful signal even for your own emerging-market exposure.

**Know what your EM bond funds hold.** If you invest in emerging-market debt, you’re exposed to these dynamics.

**If you care about the issue,** debt-relief and development charities and campaigns are where individuals can plug in.
:::

### What to watch

- The US dollar index (DXY) and US real yields: a stronger dollar mechanically raises the local-currency cost of foreign-currency debt.

- Sovereign foreign-currency bond spreads for frontier issuers, and the count of new IMF programmes or missed coupons.

- Progress (or paralysis) in G20 Common Framework restructurings — the test of whether the system can resolve rather than defer.

- Commodity and food prices: importers’ fiscal space lives and dies here.
