**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone who banks with a regional or community bank, works in a downtown economy, or owns property near struggling office districts.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Active distress, widely anticipated*

**Why this level:** Record office delinquencies and a genuine maturity wall, but the peak is easing and the risk is unusually well-telegraphed. **Raises it:** A cluster of regional-bank losses; office values falling further; ‘extend and pretend’ ending abruptly. **Lowers it:** Orderly refinancing via private credit; office-value stabilisation; the maturity peak passing.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** A huge volume of commercial real-estate (CRE) loans — roughly \$875–\$936bn, about 17% of the \$5tn US CRE debt market — comes due in 2026, much of it written in 2019–2022 at 3–4% rates and maturing into a 6–8% world, against property values that are in many cases 16–35% lower. (The 2026 figure is actually down about 9% from 2025’s ~\$957bn, so the very peak may be passing.)

**\[Established\]** Office is the epicentre: the CMBS office delinquency rate hit a record 12.34% in January 2026. Banks hold roughly \$1.89tn of CRE loans on income-producing property, with securitised lenders holding another ~\$753bn. The long-running “extend and pretend” tactic — modifying and extending loans rather than recognising losses — is starting to break as lenders finally acknowledge losses.

**\[Single-source\]** One industry analysis estimates regional banks hold about \$396bn of the maturing debt and that over half exceed the 300% CRE-to-capital level regulators watch; **\[Established\]** regulators are tightening oversight of CRE-heavy banks, and the Fed’s loan-officer survey shows banks have tightened CRE standards for eight straight quarters.

### Why it is under-covered

“Office buildings in trouble” sounds like a niche real-estate problem. The under-covered angle is the link to regional banks — which hold a disproportionate share of this debt — and therefore to local lending, small-business credit, and the deposits of ordinary people who never think about CMBS.

### The debate

**The alarmist case:** a slow-motion solvency drain on regional banks as extensions expire, values stay depressed, and losses crystallise — echoing the regional-bank scare of 2023.

**The reassuring case:** this wall is the most widely anticipated in history, so markets and banks have been provisioning and adapting (extensions, recapitalisations, private-credit takeouts); CRE loans carry less leverage and more cushion than 2008-era mortgages, and bank CRE delinquencies remain far below the ~9% peak of 2010.

**Both sides accept:** office values have fallen hard, a large slug of debt must be refinanced into much higher rates, and regional banks are the concentrated pressure point.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Because everyone sees this coming, it may resolve as a grind rather than a crash: loan quality matters more than maturity volume, well-located properties with strong tenants refinance fine, private credit fills gaps, and the maturity peak is already easing from 2025. Insured depositors are protected regardless. The vulnerability is concentration and confidence: a few regional banks with heavy office exposure and thin capital could still fail, and — as 2023 showed — fear can move faster than fundamentals once a name is in the spotlight.

### Historical parallel

The early-1990s savings-and-loan and CRE bust is the template for a drawn-out workout that takes years and shutters weak lenders without sinking the system. 2008 is the scarier comparison, but most analysts argue CRE today has lower leverage, more diverse funding, and far lower delinquencies than residential mortgages did then — a slow bleed, not a sudden break.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Lots of loans on office buildings and other commercial property were taken out when interest rates were very low. They’re now coming due all at once, but rates are much higher and many of those buildings are worth far less — especially offices. Smaller “regional” banks made a lot of these loans, so the strain lands on them. Your insured deposits are safe, but if local banks are nursing property losses, they lend less, which can squeeze small businesses and local economies. It’s more likely to be a slow grind than a sudden crash, partly because everyone has seen it coming.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Stay within deposit-insurance limits.** In the US, FDIC insurance covers $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. If you’re near that at one bank, spreading across banks (or using insured-sweep options) keeps you fully covered no matter what.

**Know your bank.** You don’t need to panic-switch, but it’s reasonable to be aware if your bank is unusually concentrated in commercial real estate; bank financial summaries are public.

**Keep some liquidity outside any single institution** so an isolated bank problem never freezes your access to cash.

**If you own affected property,** engage lenders early on extensions or restructuring — the worst outcomes hit those who wait until maturity day.
:::

### What to watch

- CMBS office delinquency and special-servicing rates — the clearest distress gauge.

- Regional-bank loan-loss provisions, CRE concentration ratios, and any rating downgrades.

- The pace of “extend and pretend” ending — rising recognised losses vs continued extensions.

- Private-credit appetite to refinance maturing CRE — the relief valve.
