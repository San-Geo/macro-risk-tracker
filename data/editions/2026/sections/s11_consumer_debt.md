**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone with a car loan, anyone who uses Buy Now Pay Later, parents of Gen Z, and anyone applying for a mortgage near someone who does.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Acute at the bottom, contained overall*

**Why this level:** Record subprime-auto stress and invisible BNPL debt at the fragile end, but small relative to the system with no clear broad spillover yet. **Raises it:** Rising unemployment; BNPL/auto delinquencies bleeding into cards and mortgages; a student-loan default surge. **Lowers it:** Stable employment and real wages; BNPL data reaching the credit bureaus; delinquencies plateauing.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Two pockets of American household stress are flashing red while the averages look calm. First, subprime auto: 60-plus-day delinquencies among subprime borrowers hit their highest level in 32 years in January 2026, repossessions rose 43% between 2022 and 2024, and auto-loan rejection rates have roughly doubled. Loan terms have stretched to 84 months and negative equity (owing more than the car is worth) is at records.

**\[Established\]** Second, “Buy Now, Pay Later” (BNPL) — instalment plans from Klarna, Affirm, Afterpay and others — which regulators (the Richmond Fed) literally call “phantom debt” because much of it isn’t reported to the main credit bureaus. A borrower can stack loans across several apps and still look debt-free on a credit report. Because BNPL balances are small, people prioritise paying them — so their larger debts (cards, car, student loans) default first, meaning official metrics understate the strain.

**\[Contested\]** BNPL’s size is genuinely murky and reported numbers conflict. The Richmond Fed estimates real BNPL transaction value around \$70bn in 2025 (~1.1% of card spending) — small. Other sources cite annual purchase volume (GMV) near \$560bn and outstanding balances above \$400bn. These measure different things (a year’s spending vs money currently owed), which is exactly why the risk is hard to size. **\[Single-source\]** Separately, one analysis reports US student-loan defaults reappearing sharply — around a million in late 2025 and 2.6 million in early 2026 — as pandemic-era forbearance ends.

### Why it is under-covered

Aggregate consumer numbers look fine — overall credit-card delinquency is stable, real incomes edged up. That average masks a sharply “K-shaped” reality where the bottom tier is in deep stress, and a whole category of borrowing (BNPL) is partly invisible to the data everyone relies on. As one investor put it, there are two possible bubbles right now and only the AI one gets the headlines.

### The debate

**The alarmists argue:** this is the subprime-mortgage playbook — risky consumer debt, sliced up and partly invisible — with record auto delinquencies and hidden BNPL stacking pointing to a coming reckoning.

**The Fed and regulators counter:** BNPL is still small relative to credit cards; much of the “record” subprime auto delinquency reflects credit-score migration (during the pandemic, many borrowers’ scores rose, shrinking the subprime pool and flattering its delinquency math); and there’s no clear evidence yet of broad systemic spillover.

**Both sides accept:** the most financially fragile households are genuinely strained, BNPL data gaps are real and growing, and these are early-warning lights even if not yet a system-wide fire.

### Stress test (the benign case)

The reassuring read is solid: BNPL charge-off rates have actually been low (under 2% in recent reporting), credit-card delinquencies are near pre-pandemic norms, real median wages rose faster than inflation in early 2026, and the subprime-auto “record” is partly a statistical artifact. The risk to that case is the hidden layer — if rising unemployment meets phantom debt and the end of student-loan forbearance, stress can accelerate quickly and show up in the larger loans first, where it does real damage.

### Historical parallel

2008 subprime is the cautionary tale invoked most — invisible, fragmented, under-measured risk — though the scale here is far smaller and the systemic plumbing different. A fairer frame is the “K-shaped” consumer of recent cycles: averages hide a widening gap, and the people at the bottom run out of buffers long before the headline data admits it.

::: box
**In plain terms**

On paper, American consumers look okay. But beneath the average, the people with the weakest finances are struggling: car-loan defaults among shaky borrowers just hit a 32-year high, and repossessions have jumped. At the same time, “pay in 4” instalment apps have let people pile up small debts that mostly don’t show on credit reports — so lenders (and the official statistics) can’t see them. People keep these small instalments current and let bigger bills slide, which hides how stretched they are. It’s not a national crisis yet, but it’s a warning light — and it’s easy to fall into personally without noticing.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Track every BNPL plan you have** in one place (a note or spreadsheet). The danger of “pay in 4” is that four small plans across four apps add up to a payment you can’t make.

**Treat BNPL as debt, not a discount.** If you couldn’t buy it with cash or on a card you’ll clear this month, pause before splitting it into instalments.

**Don’t roll negative equity into a new car loan.** Wrapping what you still owe on an old car into a new, longer loan is how people get deeply “underwater.” Keep cars longer; aim for loans of 60 months or less.

**Build a small emergency fund first** (even $500–$1,000). It’s the buffer that stops a surprise bill from becoming a late payment or a new instalment plan.

**If you have federal student loans,** confirm your repayment status now that forbearance has ended — don’t let a missed payment quietly become a default on your credit file.
:::

### What to watch

- Affirm/Klarna delinquency and charge-off disclosures — the clearest window into the invisible layer.

- Whether credit-card and auto delinquencies rise even as BNPL absorbs spending — a sign underlying stress is worse than any single metric.

- Progress on BNPL data flowing to credit bureaus (FICO’s BNPL-inclusive scores).

- Student-loan default figures and the unemployment rate — the accelerants.
