**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone who buys cars, electronics, or appliances, plus workers in manufacturing, clean energy, and defense — these minerals are in almost everything with a motor or magnet.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Coercive tool on a fragile truce*


**Why this level:** China’s processing chokepoint is real, the November 2025 pause is temporary with the April controls intact, and access can be throttled quickly. **Raises it:** Reinstated or expanded export controls; lapse of the pause; a geopolitical flashpoint over Taiwan or tech. **Lowers it:** An extended or broadened truce; non-China processing and magnet capacity coming online on schedule.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** China controls roughly 90% of global rare-earth permanent-magnet production and about 91% of refining and separation — the processing chokepoint, not just the mines. Through 2025 it built a licensing regime into a coercive instrument: April 2025 controls on seven heavy rare earths and magnets; an October 2025 expansion adding five more elements and, via Announcement 61, an extraterritorial reach over foreign goods containing Chinese-origin material; then a November 2025 pause (Announcements 70/72) that suspended the October measures but preserved the April controls and the licensing machinery.

**\[Established\]** The key insight from trade data: availability is not access. China’s overall magnet exports rose even as shipments to the US fell sharply, because supply is selectively allocated through discretionary licensing. US yttrium imports ran 17 tons across April–December 2025 versus 333 tons in the prior period; aerospace makers have rationed material. Ford halted Explorer production for a week in May 2025; European magnet prices reached up to six times Chinese levels.

### Why it is under-covered

Headlines treat each tariff truce as “resolved.” The structural reality — that the November détente is a temporary, revocable pause sitting atop intact controls, and that the real bottleneck is processing know-how that takes years to replicate — gets lost between summit news cycles.

### The debate

**The hawk view:** decouple fast; subsidise an ex-China chain across mining, separation, metallisation and magnet-making at any cost, because the leverage is intolerable.

**The realist view:** diversification is possible but slow, capital-heavy and chemistry-bound; switching costs run into billions per firm and decoupling could cut first-year operating profits 15–50% in exposed firms.

**Beijing’s framing:** conservation and national security.

**All sides accept:** the chokepoint is real, processing (not ore) is the binding constraint, and meaningful displacement is measured in years.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Diversification is genuinely underway: a US–Australia critical-minerals framework, a layered US–Japan partnership (including deep-sea rare-earth mud research), nearly \$4bn in US EXIM letters of intent, and new magnet capacity coming online in summer 2026. Substitutes (iron-nitride magnets, magnet-free motor architectures) are advancing. The benign case says prices stay elevated but supply chains adapt through triage and recycling. Its weakness: every credible analysis still puts true processing independence years away, and a single licensing freeze can outpace the build-out.

### Historical parallel

China’s 2010 rare-earth embargo against Japan is the precedent: it spurred a wave of patenting and some ex-China capacity, but did not close the gap quickly — dependence persisted for years. The deeper analogue is the 1973–74 oil shock, which is exactly why the IEA has launched a Critical Minerals Security Programme modelled on the emergency-stockpile cooperation that followed the oil embargo. The lesson: chokepoint shocks accelerate diversification but reward the holder for a long interim.

::: box
**In plain terms**

China controls about 90% of the processing for the ‘rare earth’ magnets used in EVs, electronics, wind turbines, and weapons. It has turned export licenses into a bargaining chip — supply can be quietly throttled even when the minerals technically exist. A November 2025 truce paused the harshest measures but left the controls in place, and building alternatives outside China takes years, so this leverage isn’t going away soon.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Mostly awareness:** expect occasional shortages, price bumps, or delays in products that rely on these magnets (some cars, electronics) when controls tighten.

**If you’re in an affected industry** (autos, electronics, defense, clean energy), supply-chain diversification and inventory planning matter.

**Treat single-stock ‘rare earth’ bets with the usual caution;** it’s a long-running theme, not a guaranteed winner.

**No need to stockpile anything personally** — the impact shows up as prices and availability, not a household emergency.
:::

### What to watch

- Chinese export-license approval times and volumes by element and destination — the real-time gauge of access vs availability.

- Whether the November pause is extended, expired, or reinstated (watch the late-2026 dates in the announcements).

- Heavy rare-earth prices (dysprosium, terbium, yttrium) and ex-China premiums.

- Commissioning milestones for non-China separation and magnet plants in 2026–2027 — announcements vs actual qualified output.
