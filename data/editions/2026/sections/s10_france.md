**WHO THIS HITS:** *Europeans (via bonds, banks, and the euro), and anyone globally with European exposure in their pension or funds — plus a lesson in how political gridlock becomes a financial cost.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Acute phase eased, structure fragile*

**Why this level:** February’s budget passage calmed spreads and the ECB backstop deters attacks, but the structural deficit and political fragility remain. **Raises it:** Snap elections or a government collapse; a credit-rating downgrade; the 5% deficit target slipping. **Lowers it:** Stable governance; credible consolidation; spreads holding near pre-2024 levels.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** After nearly two years of paralysis since President Macron’s 2024 snap election produced a hung parliament, France finally adopted a 2026 budget on 2 February 2026 — pushed through under Article 49.3, surviving two no-confidence votes only because Socialist lawmakers declined to bring the government down. The saga cost two prime ministers their jobs before Sébastien Lecornu’s fragile minority government got it over the line.

**\[Established\]** The budget targets a 5% deficit for 2026 (down from 5.4% in 2025); the original 4.6% target was abandoned after pension reform was frozen. Public debt sits near 114–116% of GDP — the eurozone’s third-highest after Greece and Italy — and is projected to climb toward ~125% by 2029. France remains under the EU’s excessive-deficit procedure.

**\[Established\]** The market’s verdict is the tell. The spread between French (OAT) and German (Bund) 10-year yields widened at its peak to the widest since the 2012 eurozone crisis (though well short of that crisis's highs), and at one point French borrowing costs matched or exceeded Italy’s — a once-unthinkable milestone. The rating agencies followed: Fitch (12 September 2025) and S&P (mid-October 2025) cut France to A+, and Moody's moved its Aa3 rating to a negative outlook (24 October 2025). After February’s budget passage, the risk premium eased back toward mid-2024 levels, but the structural fragility did not go away.

### Why it is under-covered (globally)

Outside Europe it reads as domestic French politics. But France is the eurozone’s second-largest economy, and roughly 55% of its government bonds are held by foreign investors — the group most sensitive to bad news. When France wobbles, it is a euro-area problem, not just a Paris one.

### The debate

**The worriers argue:** serial government collapses, a structural deficit, frozen pension reform, and deteriorating demographics mean France can’t credibly stabilise its debt; each crisis ratchets borrowing costs higher.

**The relaxed argue:** France still borrows cheaply by historical standards, has a long average debt maturity (~9 years) that blunts rate rises, deep and liquid markets, and — crucially — the ECB’s backstop (the Transmission Protection Instrument) standing behind it.

**Both sides accept:** the political system struggles to deliver consolidation, debt is rising, and the market now charges France a standing “political risk premium.”

### Stress test (why it probably doesn’t blow up soon)

The eurozone has something the 2012 crisis lacked: a credible central-bank backstop. The mere existence of the ECB’s TPI deters speculative attacks, and France’s long maturities mean higher rates feed through slowly. The real risk is not a sudden default but a slow erosion — a higher permanent interest bill that crowds out spending, plus tail scenarios (snap elections, an early end to Macron’s term) that could reprice risk faster than the backstop calms it.

### Historical parallel

The 2010–12 eurozone sovereign-debt crisis is the obvious reference, but the ECB’s post-2012 “whatever it takes” toolkit changes the ending. A sharper warning is the UK’s September 2022 gilt crisis: a credible-looking advanced economy saw its bond market seize in days once confidence cracked — proof that “it can’t happen here” is not a risk model.

**In plain terms**

France keeps struggling to agree on a budget — the fight has toppled two prime ministers — because no party has a majority. Its government debt is high and rising, and for a moment investors treated France as riskily as Italy, which is striking for a core European country. A budget finally passed in February 2026, calming markets somewhat, but the underlying problem (spending more than it raises, with politics too divided to fix it) is still there. Because France is huge and its debt is widely held abroad, its troubles are really a euro-wide issue. A full crisis is unlikely soon mainly because the European Central Bank stands ready to step in.
:::

**What you can do now**

**For most non-Europeans, this is awareness, not action** — but it’s worth checking whether your bond or “international” funds carry heavy French/eurozone government-debt exposure.

**Diversify geography.** If a large share of your savings is tied to one country or currency bloc, broadening across regions reduces single-country political risk.

**Watch the euro if you hold it or earn in it;** persistent political risk premiums can weigh on the currency over time, affecting purchasing power.

**Take the general lesson:** political gridlock has a real price (higher borrowing costs), which is a useful lens for assessing any government’s finances, including your own country’s.
:::

### What to watch

- The OAT–Bund spread (and OAT vs Italian BTP) — the live fever chart of French risk.

- Any move toward snap elections or instability around Macron’s presidency.

- Credit-rating actions and whether the 5% deficit target actually holds.

- Any signal the ECB might use (or be asked to use) its TPI backstop.
