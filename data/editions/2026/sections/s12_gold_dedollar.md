**WHO THIS HITS:** *Every saver — because it’s a signal about long-run confidence in the dollar and government debt, and it shapes the value of money you hold.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Structural signal, not a near-term threat*

**Why this level:** A real, multi-decade reserve shift, but slow-moving with no imminent trigger; more a confidence signal than an acute risk. **Raises it:** A new reserve-weaponisation event; a disorderly dollar decline; faster non-dollar trade settlement. **Lowers it:** A stable dollar reserve share; orderly diversification; renewed official demand for Treasuries.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** The world’s central banks have bought more than 4,000 tonnes of gold between 2022 and early 2026 — the largest sustained official accumulation in modern history. They bought 863 tonnes in 2025 (down from ~1,090 in 2024 only because record prices made them more selective, not less committed). Gold hit an all-time high near \$5,600/oz in late January 2026 before easing to around \$4,300 by mid-June.

**\[Established\]** Meanwhile the US dollar’s share of global currency reserves has fallen to roughly 56.8% (late 2025) from about 72% in the early 2000s — the lowest since 1994 — a steady, multi-decade drift that has continued even when the dollar was strong. The 2026 World Gold Council survey (a record 76 central banks) found 45% plan to add gold and 74% expect the dollar’s reserve share to fall further.

**\[Single-source\]** Strikingly, Morgan Stanley research notes gold now makes up a larger share of central-bank reserves than US Treasuries for the first time since 1996. **\[Established\]** Much of the buying is under-reported — China discloses only small amounts even as its gold imports run far higher — so official tallies understate the trend.

### Why it is under-covered

Reserve management is the dullest-sounding topic in finance, and “gold price hits record” gets covered as a market story, not a monetary one. The real signal — the people who manage national wealth quietly reducing dollar dependence and removing counterparty risk — is a slow architectural shift that rarely makes front pages until it’s far advanced.

### The debate

**The “de-dollarization” camp argues:** this is a structural turn away from a dollar that has been “weaponised” through sanctions; gold can’t be frozen or inflated by another government, so sovereigns are buying insurance against US financial power and debt.

**The “just diversification” camp (incl. Fed research) argues:** the dollar still dwarfs all rivals at ~57% of reserves, no currency can replace it, and gold buying is prudent hedging — not the end of the dollar system.

**Both sides accept:** the dollar’s reserve share has fallen for 25 years, central-bank gold demand is at historic highs, and no single currency is filling the gap the dollar is vacating.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Nothing here implies a dollar collapse. The dollar remains dominant in trade invoicing, debt issuance, and payments; reserve shifts unfold over decades; and a 57% share is still overwhelming. Gold buying can simply be sensible diversification after a generation of over-concentration. The caution is directional: reserve-currency transitions historically show up in the data long before they register in public consciousness, and a steady bid for gold over Treasuries is the same “who will buy government debt?” question from Section 1, seen from the official-sector side.

### Historical parallel

The sterling-to-dollar handover across the first half of the 20th century is the model for how slowly reserve status changes — measured in decades, not quarters. The 1970s breakdown of the gold-anchored Bretton Woods system is the reminder that monetary regimes do shift, and that gold tends to be where capital hides when faith in paper claims wavers.

::: box
**In plain terms**

The institutions that manage countries’ savings — central banks — have been buying gold at the fastest pace in modern history and slowly holding fewer dollars. They started in earnest after the US froze Russia’s reserves in 2022, which taught everyone that dollars held abroad can be switched off. This doesn’t mean the dollar is finished — it’s still by far the most-used currency — but it’s a quiet vote of reduced confidence in holding everything in dollars and government IOUs. For an ordinary saver, it’s a signal worth noting about the long-run value of cash and debt, and why a little diversification can make sense.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Understand gold’s role before buying any.** It’s a diversifier and a hedge against currency and crisis risk — not a guaranteed money-maker. It pays no income and can fall sharply (it already dropped from ~$5,600 to ~$4,300 in months).

**Keep any allocation modest.** Many advisers think of single-digit percentages of a portfolio; concentrating heavily in gold is its own risk.

**Beware markups and scams.** Physical-gold dealers can charge large premiums, and “rare coin” pitches are a classic trap. Low-cost, well-known gold funds avoid storage and authenticity headaches; if you want physical, compare premiums and use reputable dealers.

**Take the broader hint:** diversifying across currencies and real assets is a reasonable response to long-run concerns about debt and inflation — gold is one option among several, not a silver bullet.
:::

### What to watch

- The IMF COFER data on the dollar’s reserve share, released quarterly.

- World Gold Council quarterly demand reports and the annual central-bank survey.

- Whether large holders (especially China) report more of their actual buying.

- Any moves toward non-dollar trade settlement among major economies.
