**WHO THIS HITS:** *Every homeowner and renter — especially in disaster-exposed areas — plus anyone with a mortgage or hoping to buy.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Building, with household-level damage now*

**Why this level:** Premiums and non-renewals are rising and the value-and-mortgage channel is real, but the systemic transmission is still gradual. **Raises it:** A major catastrophe season; accelerating insurer exits; home-value declines in high-risk zones. **Lowers it:** Successful mitigation and pricing reforms; reinsurance capacity returning; credible policy backstops.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Property insurers are raising premiums sharply, declining to renew policies, and exiting whole regions as climate-driven disaster losses mount — not just in California and Florida, but in places like New England that aren’t thought of as high-risk. US insured catastrophe losses reached \$112.7bn in 2024, up 36% year-on-year. A Consumer Federation analysis found roughly 1 in 13 US homeowners now uninsured, leaving about \$1.6tn in home value unprotected.

**\[Established\]** This is becoming a financial-stability issue, not just a household-budget one. Because mortgage lenders require insurance, when coverage becomes unaffordable or unavailable, home sales fall through, values drop, and — as the Dallas Fed has documented — mortgage delinquencies and defaults rise. A US Senate Budget Committee investigation described the trajectory as “2008 all over again,” except potentially more permanent: a flooded-out or uninsurable home may never recover its value.

**\[Single-source\]** Supporting figures to treat as leads: a 2026 survey found nearly half of home buyers/sellers hit insurance problems and 21% had a deal fall through; in Louisiana, 30–40% of mortgage applications reportedly fail over insurance costs; premiums rose around 20% over two years. **\[Established\]** State “FAIR plans” — insurers of last resort — now operate in 34 states plus DC, and are absorbing more risk as private insurers pull back.

### Why it is under-covered

It’s reported as a personal-finance gripe — “my premium went up” — rather than as a systemic threat to the housing market and the financial system. The mechanism that turns rising premiums into falling home values and mortgage defaults is the under-told part.

### The debate

**The financial-stability camp argues:** this is a slow-building 2008-style risk — uninsurable homes lose value, mortgages sour, and losses ripple through lenders and local tax bases.

**The market-signal camp argues:** rising premiums are climate risk being priced correctly; suppressing prices or over-relying on state insurers of last resort just hides the risk and pushes the cost onto everyone else.

**Both sides accept:** premiums are rising fast, availability is shrinking, more homes are uninsured, and the mortgage market is exposed wherever insurance becomes unavailable.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Insurance markets adapt: new pricing models, parametric products, reinsurance, mitigation incentives, and stronger building codes can restore coverage in many areas, and most of the country is not at the extreme tail of risk. The vulnerability is that the worst-hit areas may be genuinely, permanently uninsurable, and that the damage concentrates on lower-income and minority households least able to absorb it — turning a market adjustment into a localized wealth shock.

### Historical parallel

The comparison everyone reaches for is 2008 — a housing-finance shock building quietly until it cascades. The crucial difference, and why some call it worse, is permanence: 2008 property values eventually rebounded, but a home that can’t be insured against a recurring disaster may not. Observers also note the map of uninsurable areas can echo historical redlining, concentrating harm on the same communities.

**In plain terms**

Insuring a home is getting much more expensive, and in some places you can’t get insurance at all, because disasters are causing record losses. This matters beyond your bill: you generally can’t get a mortgage without insurance, so when coverage disappears, buyers vanish, home values fall, and more people miss mortgage payments. Experts warn this could hit the housing market the way the 2008 crisis did — but possibly more lastingly, because an uninsurable home may never regain its value. It’s spreading beyond the obvious places like Florida and California.
:::

**What you can do now**

**Don’t go uninsured to save money.** An uninsured disaster can wipe out your largest asset. If premiums are painful, first compare quotes widely — prices vary a lot between insurers.

**Raise your deductible thoughtfully** to lower premiums if (and only if) you have the savings to cover that deductible after a loss.

**Invest in mitigation — it can cut premiums and keep you insurable.** Roof upgrades, defensible space against wildfire, storm shutters, and water-leak sensors are often rewarded by insurers and sometimes subsidised by state programs.

**Document your home now.** Photos/video of every room and major possession, stored in the cloud, make claims far easier and fairer after a disaster.

**Before buying a home,** get an insurance quote before you commit — not after — and check the area’s disaster history and whether insurers are still writing new policies there. Know whether a state FAIR plan is your only option.

**Review your coverage limits** against today’s rebuilding costs; many policies are under-insured after years of construction inflation.
:::

### What to watch

- Insurer non-renewal and market-exit announcements by state; growth in FAIR-plan enrollment.

- The Dallas Fed / academic data linking insurance costs to mortgage delinquencies.

- Home-value softening in high-risk zip codes — the early sign of the value channel biting.

- Federal or state policy responses (backstops, mitigation funding, disclosure rules).
