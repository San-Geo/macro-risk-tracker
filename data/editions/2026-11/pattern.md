Read together across this edition, the twenty-one stories are no longer five loosely coupled layers — they are showing signs of active, simultaneous co-movement for the first time since this brief began. The mechanism is simpler than it looks: a physical chokepoint (Story 17) generated an oil price shock; that shock produced inflation that could not be solved by monetary policy; the Fed hiked into a supply shock anyway, lifting the rate level across the curve; and that higher rate level is now stressing every interest-rate-sensitive story in the brief simultaneously. That is the new architecture of risk in this edition.

Follow the chain. The Strait of Hormuz closure (Story 17) removed roughly a quarter of global oil flows [Established]. A complete cessation of oil exports from the Persian Gulf corresponds to a disruption of roughly 20% of global oil supplies, which the Dallas Fed's model estimated would raise US headline inflation by 1.7 percentage points annualised in the first quarter of 2026. That is a supply problem that rate hikes cannot fix. The Fed hiked regardless in September, pushing the effective policy rate to 3.75%–4.00% [Established]. That hike, combined with the energy-inflation premium already embedded in long-end yields, pushed the 10-year Treasury above 5% (Story 1) and the MOVE index above 100 [Established]. The MOVE surge widened the basis trade's risk profile; the higher rate level directly increased debt-service costs for floating-rate private credit borrowers (Story 3), which is why that story's default rate more than doubled to 6.3% in a single year [Established]. The same rate level is the ceiling against which \$1.26 trillion in CRE debt must refinance in 2027 (Story 7) [Single-source], and is part of the mechanism widening France's OAT–Bund spread (Story 11), since French debt carries a long duration and a high refinancing need.

Analysts have noted that contagion in European government bonds has switched direction — it is now France driving spread pressure onto the periphery, rather than the periphery driving pressure onto France. For now, the risk of market stress escalating sharply and spreading to the real economy appears limited, with better-capitalised banks and low household debt helping contain contagion. That is the honest qualification: the stresses are real but not yet cascading. The ECB backstop, better-capitalised European banks, and the absence of a full sovereign funding crisis are material buffers. Similarly, the US private credit system, while under growing strain, has not yet experienced a liquidity event that forces mark-to-market losses into the open. The basis trade's SOFR–EFFR widening is mild. The CRE maturity wall is a 2027 problem that is not today's problem.

What has changed is the *combination* rather than any single indicator. Six months ago, high Treasury yields, private credit stress, France's fiscal deterioration, and the Gulf closure were each a separate, containable concern. Today they share a common mechanism — the rate level — and are therefore harder to address one at a time. Every tool that might ease one tends to worsen another: rate cuts that would relieve private credit borrowers (Story 3) and the CRE wall (Story 7) would also risk re-inflating the oil-driven price level (Story 17) and weakening the dollar (Story 12). Fiscal stimulus that might offset the energy-driven demand shock is constrained by the spreads that stimulus would itself widen (Story 11). This is the loss-of-firefighting-capacity problem that the previous edition identified in the abstract — it is now visible in the data.

The physical layer (Section 5) and the geoeconomic layer (Section 4) remain the upstream generators of these stresses: the Hormuz closure did not emerge from a financial model, it emerged from a geopolitical decision. The rare-earth extension (Story 15), the ASML export controls (Story 16), and Tehran's explicitly documented plans to attack undersea cables if struck (Story 17) are similarly not market events — they are state actions that arrive on political timetables and cannot be hedged away by the financial system. The food and fertiliser fragility (Story 21) and the power-grid shortfall (Story 18) are arithmetic constraints that will bind harder as the AI build-out accelerates.

The honest synthesis for this edition: the system has moved from many separate elevated risks to several risks with a common driver. That common driver — higher long rates sustained by a supply shock the central bank cannot cure — is the thread that, if pulled, connects the leverage (Section 1), the credit marks (Section 2), and the sovereign backstop (Section 3) into a single loop. The tracker's overall rise from 6.5 to 7.8 reflects exactly this: not that any single story has become catastrophic, but that more stories are now correlated with each other than they were six months ago, which is precisely the condition under which a single shock can light several floors at once.

### Connections to watch

- **One rate level, five pressure points.** The same higher-for-longer rate environment that widened the Treasury basis trade (Story 1), doubled private credit default rates (Story 3), stalled CRE refinancing (Story 7), pushed France's OAT yield toward 5% (Story 11), and increased developing-world debt-service costs (Story 10) has a single upstream cause — an oil supply shock (Story 17) that monetary policy can dampen but cannot cure. Watching the 10-year Treasury yield and the MOVE index together tells you how tightly coupled these stories currently are.

- **The Gulf closure reaches the balance sheet through the food chain.** The Strait of Hormuz disruption (Story 17) is not only a trade and energy story: higher diesel and shipping costs transmit directly into fertiliser input prices (Story 21), which lifts the FAO Food Price Index and adds to the same headline inflation that prompted the Fed's September hike — which in turn raised floating-rate private credit borrowers' debt-service costs (Story 3). The household that is already behind on car payments (Story 8) is also paying more at the fuel pump and the supermarket checkout.

- **France is now the contagion source, not the contagion destination.** Whereas France previously fell victim to contagion from the eurozone periphery, the direction has now reversed. Early signs of contagion are visible beyond France to other European sovereigns, with Italian government bond yields widening materially and Spain and other peripheral sovereigns also weakening — meaning Story 11 is no longer contained and has become a driver of the broader monetary-order risks tracked in Story 10 and the rate conditions that underpin Story 1.

- **Private credit sits on both sides of the AI bet, and the Fed hike has tightened both sides simultaneously.** Story 3 treats AI as a threat to private-credit borrowers disrupted by AI; Story 6 shows private credit funding AI data centres through asset-backed loans. The September rate hike (prompted by the Gulf shock in Story 17) raised debt-service costs for software-sector borrowers and for data-centre borrowers at the same moment — hitting the same lenders twice through two different loan books.

- **Energy spikes are a clearinghouse event, not merely a volatility event.** The 2022 gas and nickel spikes demonstrated that commodity price surges generate margin calls large enough to threaten clearinghouse members' liquidity. An oil and gas shock from the Gulf (Story 17) running through commodity clearing models is therefore a direct trigger for Story 5, not merely an indirect one through general volatility. The MOVE index's rise above 100 (Story 1) and the VIX's rise to 21.46 (Story 5) are the early-warning readings; the commodity-margin channel is the more acute potential path.

- **China holds three levers simultaneously, and the Gulf shock has complicated all three.** China's bifurcated price picture — deflation in consumer goods, PPI at +5.0% year-on-year driven by energy costs (Story 14) — means that higher Gulf oil prices (Story 17) are now partly *offsetting* the deflationary export pressure that was the original risk. Meanwhile, China's rare-earth extension (Story 15) and the ASML chokepoint (Story 16) remain intact. An escalation in the chip contest can still arrive as a minerals shock; both feed the trade-retaliation cycle; and the energy cost transmission from the Gulf now makes China's own export economics harder to predict.

- **The same physical constraints that cap AI revenue growth also cap the grid that powers AI.** Hyperscaler capex is growing at over 100% year-on-year (Story 6) while PJM's capacity auction produced the grid's first reliability shortfall for the 2028/29 delivery year (Story 18). The circular financing in Story 6 assumes continued AI revenue growth; that growth requires data-centre power the grid cannot yet guarantee. A grid curtailment event would therefore be simultaneously a Story 18 physical risk and a Story 6 financial risk, arriving as a single event on two sections of this brief at once.
