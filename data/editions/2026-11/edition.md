# What changed since the last edition {#what-changed}

The most consequential shift since the last edition is the convergence of three previously separate threat streams — an energy supply shock, a sovereign debt flare-up, and a private-credit deterioration — into a single, mutually reinforcing pressure on long-end interest rates. That common driver explains why the overall tracker has moved from 6.5 to 7.8 and why four stories have changed band.

The single largest move belongs to **Story 3 (Private credit)**, which jumped from 6 to 8. The Fitch US Private Credit Default Rate more than doubled in nine months to a record 6.3%, redemption gates at semi-liquid funds became routine rather than exceptional, and the Fed's September rate hike directly raised debt-service costs for borrowers who are already stretched [Established]. That hike was itself partly a response to the oil-price shock flowing from **Story 17 (Sea lanes)**, which moved from 6 to 7: the Strait of Hormuz has remained effectively shut for Day 246 as of 2 November, pushing WTI crude to \$96.10 — a level that, as the Dallas Fed's modelling confirmed, is consistent with a closure now exceeding two quarters [Established]. **Story 1 (Treasury basis trade)** moved from 6 to 7 as the MOVE index surged past 100 and the 10-year Treasury yield briefly touched 5.34% on 1 October — its highest since 2002 — driven partly by the same energy-inflation dynamic [Established]. **Story 5 (Clearinghouses)** moved from 4 to 5: the VIX and MOVE index both rose, raising the probability of a procyclical margin episode [Established]. **Story 7 (CRE / regional banks)** moved from 5 to 6 as CMBS office delinquency crossed into a higher stress band and the pace of bank failures accelerated [Established].

The common driver threading through all five upgrades is a higher-for-longer rate environment that was not the base case six months ago: it stresses private credit borrowers (Story 3), loads the Treasury basis trade (Story 1), widens sovereign spreads in France (Story 11), and makes the CRE refinancing wall steeper (Story 7). The Gulf disruption is the upstream cause of the rate shock; the rest of the system is the transmission.

# How to read this brief {#how-to-read}

**What this is.** Twenty-one under-covered risks to the global financial and economic system, each written to be readable by a non-specialist and useful to a professional. They were chosen because they are consequential, connected, and under-discussed relative to their importance.

**How it is organized.** The stories are grouped into five mechanism-based sections — Funding & Leverage; Credit, Consumers & Valuations; Sovereigns & the Monetary Order; Geoeconomics & Trade Weapons; and Physical & Resource Limits. Grouping by mechanism, rather than by headline, is what makes the connections between stories visible, and it is the same structure the live tracker uses to score risk.

**How to read each story.** Every story opens with who it affects and a threat meter (1–10, from Low to Severe), then explains what is happening, why it is under-covered, the genuine debate on both sides, a stress test, a historical parallel, a plain-English summary, and what an ordinary person can reasonably do.

**A note on the levels.** The 1–10 score measures vulnerability — the potential to do systemic damage if stressed — not the probability of a near-term blow-up. A high number is a reason to watch, not to panic. The levels are the analyst’s baseline judgments on the compile date. The live tracker starts from these baselines and moves them with its indicators, so its readings can differ: on its first full day (30 June 2026) several stories already read one level higher.

### Confidence tags

**\[Established\]** widely documented by multiple authoritative sources.

**\[Contested\]** real, but debated in size or interpretation.

**\[Single-source\]** reported but not yet widely corroborated.

**\[Forecast\]** a forward projection, not a present fact.

# Section 1 — Funding & Leverage {#section-1}

*Funding & Leverage (Stories 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)*

These five stories share a single mechanism: leverage that lives in the plumbing of the financial system, funded by someone's very short-term money. The Treasury basis trade, Japan's carry trade, private credit, stablecoins, and the clearinghouses that sit behind them are rarely discussed together, but they fail the same way — through funding that can evaporate faster than the assets it supports can be sold.

What has changed since the last edition is that the rate environment has turned against the whole section simultaneously. The 10-year Treasury yield briefly touched 5.34%, the MOVE index surpassed 100, and the Fed's September hike pushed floating-rate private credit borrowers further into distress — all while private credit's default rate more than doubled to a record. These are not independent data points: they are the same higher-for-longer shock expressing itself across different plumbing. The SOFR–EFFR spread, though still modest at 0.07%, has widened, signalling that repo funding conditions are beginning to feel the strain [Established]. The SEC's 30 October exemptive relief for certain repo transactions resolves one edge-case ambiguity in the clearing mandate without loosening its core [Established]. The basis trade and the clearinghouses remain mechanically coupled: the same positions moving into central clearing are the ones that must be margined when volatility spikes. Private credit is the slow-motion cousin — less marked-to-market but directly exposed to the rate level that is now rising. What ties the set together is reflexivity: a shock to funding costs forces selling that worsens the very prices that triggered it.

## 1. The Treasury “basis trade”: hidden leverage under the world’s safest asset {#s1_basis_trade}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone with savings, a mortgage, or a pension — this sits under the US Treasury market, which sets the price of almost all borrowing and most investments.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 7/10 — High** *Leverage high, volatility elevated*


**Since the last edition:** Two key indicators moved into a higher band between late June and early November 2026. Treasury volatility, as measured by the MOVE index, climbed from a calm 66.79 at the last edition to above 100 by late October — the MOVE index rose significantly in this period, decoupling from equity volatility in an unusual pattern, quite different from the March spike that hit both markets simultaneously. Separately, the 10-year Treasury yield moved above 5% for the first time since 2023 in mid-September and briefly touched 5.34% on 1 October, its highest since 2002. The SOFR–EFFR spread also widened, from 0.03% to 0.07% as of 30 October, signalling mild additional strain in repo funding conditions. On the structural side, the SEC redrawn a part of the Treasury repo clearing perimeter on 30 October, issuing final exemptive relief for a capped amount of non-US client repo and expanding the inter-affiliate exclusion — the wider clearing mandate remains intact, but its cross-border edge is now less blunt. Together these shifts pushed the threat level from Elevated (6/10) to High (7/10).

**Why this level:** Among the largest hidden-leverage points in markets; Treasury volatility has now climbed to a level historically associated with stress episodes, repo funding costs are edging higher, and central clearing — the main structural fix — is still weeks away for cash and months away for repo. **Raises it:** A further spike in Treasury volatility or repo rates forcing simultaneous margin calls across leveraged funds; the clearing carve-out weakening the mandate's scope; ongoing yield pressure from record debt issuance. **Lowers it:** Central clearing implemented on schedule at end-2026; Treasury volatility retreating to the mid-60s range seen in early 2026; a further voluntary reduction in basis-trade leverage.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** A small group of highly leveraged hedge funds has become a central marginal player in the $30tn US Treasury market through the cash-futures basis trade — buying cash Treasuries, shorting Treasury futures, and financing the position in the overnight repo market. As of early 2026, hedge funds held around $2 trillion in Treasuries overall, representing a record 7% of the entire Treasury market.

**\[Contested\]** Estimates of the trade's size have shifted since the last edition. By mid-2026, the total leverage associated with the trade had swelled to around $1 trillion, before hedge funds pulled back more than $200 billion in positions in subsequent months. Assets tied to leveraged basis strategies fell about 20% in 2026 to roughly $1.2 trillion, as higher rates, tighter price differences and changing market conditions reduced potential returns. **\[Established\]** What is not disputed: leverage is still concentrated and high. The Federal Reserve's Financial Stability Report released in May explicitly noted that hedge-fund leverage remains near historical highs and is highly concentrated among large funds, warning that if fund financing channels are suddenly disrupted, high leverage could trigger risk spillovers.

**\[Established\]** The SEC's mandate to centrally clear cash Treasuries and Treasury repo remains the regulators' main structural response. The compliance date for cash Treasury transactions is approaching on 31 December 2026, with repo transactions following on 30 June 2027; daily cleared Treasury volumes are already approximately 165% higher than before the Commission's proposal. **\[Single-source\]** On 30 October 2026, just days before this edition's data date, the SEC issued final exemptive relief allowing a capped amount of otherwise in-scope non-US client repo to remain bilateral and expanding the inter-affiliate exclusion — the wider clearing mandate remains intact, but its cross-border edge is now less blunt. The compliance date for cash Treasury transactions was not moved; the SEC does not currently intend to extend these deadlines.

**\[Established\]** MOVE index readings above 100 are the level at which basis-trade margin and haircut pressures historically become self-reinforcing. The MOVE index climbed sharply in October 2026, above 110 at its peak, while the VIX stayed near 15–16, an unusual decoupling.

### Why it is under-covered

It is pure market plumbing with no retail hook, no villain, and a name that signals "technical." Yet it sits beneath the asset that prices everything else on Earth. When it breaks, it does so violently and the public only learns the term afterwards. The bond-volatility surge of October 2026 received far less mainstream attention than concurrent equity moves, even as MOVE outpaced VIX by a historically unusual margin.

### The debate

**Regulators argue:** the trade is large, concentrated, funded by ultra-short repo, and exposed to a doom loop — a volatility spike raises futures margins and repo haircuts at once, forcing funds to dump cash Treasuries into a falling market, widening the very basis they were arbitraging. Regulators warn that hedge funds' heavy leverage — particularly in Treasury cash-futures basis trades, where leverage can exceed 20× — could trigger forced deleveraging and liquidity crises under market stress.

**The industry argues:** the trade is functionally useful — it keeps futures and cash prices aligned, adds auction demand and liquidity in normal times — and concentration is declining as spreads compress. There is currently no clear evidence of a broader deterioration in hedge-fund demand for Treasuries or systemic stress across the basis trade, according to some bank strategists. Advocates note that hedge funds' willingness to trade rather than simply hold bonds to maturity can provide two-sided liquidity during both rallies and sell-offs, which could ultimately stabilise rate moves and reduce volatility.

**Both sides accept:** the position is genuinely large, genuinely concentrated in a handful of funds, central to Treasury functioning, and that central clearing is imminent. The disagreement is only about how it behaves under acute stress.

### Stress test (the benign case)

The optimistic read has two planks. First, hedge funds have scaled back Treasury basis positions as shrinking price discrepancies have made the strategy less attractive, with notional value falling to about $900bn from approximately $1.26tn at the start of 2026 — a smaller position is a smaller potential disorderly unwind. Second, the industry has made significant strides in preparation for the implementation dates, extended to 31 December 2026 for cash Treasury clearing and 30 June 2027 for Treasury repo, meaning the structural fix is now weeks rather than years away. The weakness in that case: MOVE above 100 is precisely the zone where margin and haircut pressures compound; the Fed's Standing Repo Facility remains untested at scale during a true panic; and the new repo carve-out leaves some cross-border positions outside the clearing net.

### Historical parallel

March 2020: basis-trade unwinding was a major contributor to the Treasury market dysfunction that forced the Fed into massive emergency purchases. Further back, the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management showed how a relative-value arbitrage on tiny spreads, levered enormously, can threaten the system when the spread moves the "wrong" way and convergence arrives too late to meet today's margin call. Both episodes ended with official intervention, not market self-correction. The October 2026 episode of MOVE rising above 110 while equity volatility stayed subdued rhymes with an early-warning pattern that preceded both events — bond stress that equity markets initially ignored.

::: box
**In plain terms**

A handful of hedge funds have borrowed enormous sums to run a razor-thin-margin trade in US government bonds — the safest, most important market in the world. In calm times it helps the market work. But between July and October 2026, Treasury volatility climbed sharply back above the 100-mark, the level at which these trades historically come under the most pressure. The funds have partially retreated — positions are smaller than earlier in the year — and mandatory central clearing is now weeks away for the cash market. But the system is not yet fixed, and a further lurch in yields could still force simultaneous rushed selling into a market that is already on edge.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Keep an emergency cash cushion.** If the bond market seizes up, everything wobbles for a while; cash means you aren't forced to sell at the worst moment.

**Don't assume "bonds are always safe."** In a 2020-style episode, even US Treasuries briefly fell; spreading across asset types matters more than owning any one 'safe' thing.

**Favour simple, low-cost, broad funds** over leveraged or exotic bond products you don't fully understand.

**Check the duration in your fixed-income holdings.** With long-dated yields near multi-decade highs and volatility elevated, longer-duration bonds carry more price risk than usual; understanding what you own matters.

**Mostly, this is awareness.** It's a plumbing risk you can't personally fix — but knowing why a sudden bond-market air-pocket can happen helps you avoid panic-selling into one.
:::

### What to watch

- The MOVE index: it reached above 110 in October 2026 and was still near 100 at the data date; a sustained move back above 110–120 would be a clear escalation signal for basis-trade stress.

- The SOFR–EFFR spread and repo rates at quarter-ends: the spread has already shifted from 0.03% to 0.07%; further widening would signal tightening dealer balance-sheet capacity and rising funding costs for the trade.

- SEC Treasury-clearing implementation milestones: the cash-market deadline of 31 December 2026 is the single most important near-term event; any further delay or material carve-out would leave the vulnerability structurally intact.

- Form PF and OFR repo data showing whether the voluntary deleveraging of 2026 continues or reverses, particularly in longer-duration basis positions which remain active even as shorter-tenor trades wind down.

## 2. Japan’s \$2.3tn bet, the JGB market, and the carry-trade fuse {#s2_japan}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone invested in global stocks or bonds — a sudden unwind of Japan’s ‘carry trade’ can jolt markets worldwide, as it briefly did in August 2024.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 7/10 — High** *Live, time-sensitive repricing*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#E8710A"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```


**Since the last edition:** The 30-year JGB yield has eased back slightly to around 3.91% (from a peak above 4%), while the 10-year yield has pulled back to roughly 2.77% as of 2 November 2026, reflecting a BoJ hold at its October meeting; the Takaichi administration's fiscal-financing path remains unspecified, keeping the underlying pressure unchanged.

**Why this level:** JGB yields at multi-decade highs, a huge new spending plan with unclear financing, and a recent (August 2024) carry-unwind precedent make this the most time-sensitive story in Section 1. **Raises it:** A sharp yen surge alongside rising JGB yields; messy financing of the Takaichi plan; a hawkish BoJ surprise. **Lowers it:** Orderly yield rises with a stable yen; a credible funding path for the plan; calm global carry conditions.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** On 24 June 2026, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi unveiled a long-term plan to mobilise more than ¥370tn (about \$2.3tn) of public and private investment over 14 years to March 2041, with ¥101.6tn earmarked for AI and semiconductors. Government is expected to fund a little under half. The plan landed with Japanese equities euphoric — the Nikkei 225 briefly topped 70,000 for the first time this month — and bond strategists uneasy.

**\[Established\]** The bond market context is the real story. The Bank of Japan has been normalising after decades of ultra-loose policy: it ended yield-curve control and negative rates, is running quantitative tightening, and on 16 June 2026 raised its policy rate to 1.0%, the highest since 1995. Long-end yields have repriced sharply — the 30-year JGB broke above 4% for the first time in May 2026 and the 10-year yield peaked above 3.1% in early October 2026 before pulling back to around 2.77% by 2 November [Established] — and life insurers, the traditional buyers of super-long bonds, have been trimming exposure, prompting Japan’s FSA to accelerate a review of unrealised losses on their balance sheets.

**\[Single-source\]** One market write-up puts Japan’s debt-to-GDP near 232% for end-2026; **\[Established\]** the government’s own three fiscal scenarios show the debt ratio declining only in the optimistic case and rising again in the 2030s otherwise. Bloomberg’s same-week assessment was blunt: the plan “raises fresh JGB concerns.”

### Why it is under-covered (in the West)

It reads as a domestic Japanese fiscal story. But Japan is the world’s largest creditor nation, and the yen is the world’s funding currency. The transmission belt from Tokyo’s bond market to global asset prices runs through the carry trade — and that belt is rarely on Western front pages until it snaps.

### The debate

**The bull case:** decisive industrial policy (AI, chips, defence, shipbuilding) finally breaks Japan out of stagnation; reflation plus growth shrinks the debt ratio; equity markets are voting yes.

**The bond-market case:** the plan’s financing is unspecified, issuance pressure meets a BoJ that is buying less, and rising domestic yields plus a stronger yen make Japanese institutions repatriate capital.

**Both sides accept:** the plan is unprecedented in scale, the financing path is genuinely unclear, and long-end JGB yields are structurally higher than at any point in a generation.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Japan funds itself almost entirely domestically, in its own currency, with a vast pool of household and institutional savings — the textbook reason a 230%-plus debt ratio has never triggered a crisis. If reflation is real and wages keep rising, higher nominal growth makes the debt easier, not harder, to carry, and insurers eventually become natural buyers of higher-yielding super-longs again. The vulnerability in that case is timing and speed: an orderly repricing is benign; a disorderly one that coincides with a yen surge forces a synchronised global carry unwind.

### Historical parallel

August 2024 is the live warning: a modest BoJ hike plus yen strength triggered a violent, if brief, global carry-trade unwind that hit equities worldwide. Late 2022–early 2023 saw global bond markets wobble the last time JGB yields jumped meaningfully. The pattern is consistent — yen-funded leverage built quietly over years can reverse in days.

::: box
**In plain terms**

For decades Japan had near-zero interest rates, so investors borrowed cheaply in yen to buy higher-returning assets all over the world — the ‘carry trade.’ Now Japan’s rates and bond yields are rising and it has just announced a giant spending plan. If the yen jumps and Japanese money heads home, that global trade can unwind fast, yanking money out of markets everywhere in a matter of days. A mini-version of exactly this happened in August 2024.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Expect occasional sharp, short drops** from this kind of unwind — and don’t panic-sell into them; historically they’ve been violent but brief.

**Keep a cash buffer** so a sudden global wobble doesn’t force your hand at the bottom.

**Diversify globally** rather than concentrating in the assets most reliant on cheap-yen funding (high-flying tech, some emerging markets).

**If you hold Japan or currency-hedged funds,** understand that a stronger yen changes how they behave.
:::

### What to watch

- USD/JPY (now around 157.9, down from 161.3 at the last edition) together with the 10- and 30-year JGB yields: rapid yen strengthening plus rising yields is the classic carry-unwind signature. [Established]

- Japanese investors’ net flows in foreign bonds (especially US Treasuries): repatriation removes a major marginal buyer of US debt — directly linking this story to Story 1.

- BoJ meeting guidance and the pace of QT — no BoJ meeting is scheduled for November 2026 [Established] — and any sign the Ministry of Finance must lean on issuance to fund the plan.

- Life-insurer disclosures and FSA findings on unrealised bond losses.

## 3. Private credit’s quiet \$2tn expansion — and its first cracks {#s3_private_credit}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone with money in pensions, insurance products, or 'alternative' funds — private credit has quietly spread into retirement and insurance portfolios.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 8/10 — High** *Record defaults, gates now routine*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#E8710A"></span><span style="background:#E8710A"></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** Three developments moved the needle since 29 June 2026. First, Fitch's US Private Credit Default Rate (PCDR) hit a record 6.3% for the trailing 12 months ended Q3 2026 — the fourth consecutive quarterly increase — up from 2.73% at the time of the last edition and more than double the long-run historical average. Second, redemption gates at large semi-liquid funds became a recurring, not isolated, event: one flagship fund capped withdrawals at 5% of net asset value for a third consecutive quarter in Q3 2026, with investors seeking exit at roughly twice the permitted rate. Third, on 16 September 2026 the Federal Reserve raised interest rates by 25 basis points to a target range of 3.75%–4.00% — the first hike since 2023 — directly increasing debt-service costs for floating-rate private credit borrowers already under pressure.

**Why this level:** Defaults have now broken decisively above historical norms and are confirmed by a record reading; redemption pressure at major funds has become structural rather than episodic; and a fresh rate rise adds cost pressure to an already stressed borrower pool. **Raises it:** The default rate climbing for four straight quarters with stressed maturity extensions and payment-in-kind arrangements masking the true extent of impairment; gates at funds representing combined assets of roughly $135 billion; a Fed that may hike again; and the insurer channel still opaque. **Lowers it:** Fund-level leverage remains low relative to pre-2008 securitisation structures; lock-ups prevent depositor-style bank-run dynamics; the software sector — once the most feared default source — had the lowest quarterly default rate (0.6%) in Q3; and bank capital buffers remain above stress-test minima.

---

### What is happening

**[Established]** Private credit — non-bank direct lending to mid-sized companies — has grown to roughly $1.5–$2.0 trillion in assets and sits largely outside the prudential regulatory perimeter. The Financial Stability Board has warned that the sector's performance has never been tested in an economic downturn or during a period of prolonged market stress. It cautioned that "valuation opacity and reliance on private credit ratings can amplify strains in stress," noting that the lack of public ratings makes it difficult for regulators to develop a comprehensive view of the market.

**[Established]** The default picture has deteriorated sharply since the last edition. The default rate for US private credit hit a record high in the third quarter; for the trailing 12-month period it came in at 6.3% — the fourth straight quarterly increase. Fitch recorded 27 private credit default events from 20 new unique defaulters in Q3 2026, pushing the trailing 12-month total to 91 unique defaulters. Stressed maturity extensions, interest deferrals, and payment-in-kind introductions continued to account for most default events. Notably, the industrials and manufacturing sector and consumer products and services sector had the highest default rates at 9.7% each, while the software sector — long cited as the most exposed to AI disruption — had the lowest quarterly rate at just 0.6%.

**[Established]** The rate environment has tightened further. On 16 September 2026, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates by 25 basis points to a target range of 3.75%–4.00%, the first hike since July 2023. Companies that borrow through direct lending typically carry floating-rate debt, meaning their interest costs rise automatically whenever the Fed moves rates — and the hike pushes up borrowing expenses at a moment when defaults are already climbing to levels not seen before.

**[Established]** Redemption gates at large funds have become a defining feature of this cycle, not a one-off. In Q3 2026, one major fund opened a new tender offer; investors estimated to have requested approximately 10% of shares outstanding were told the fund would fulfil only 5%, with final repurchase values expected in November. That fund hit its redemption cap for three consecutive quarters. Separately, a $31 billion fund capped redemptions at 5% in Q3 2026 after requests reached 16% — its first gate — while another $25 billion fund prorated for a second consecutive quarter after requests of approximately 11.5%.

**[Established]** The most under-watched channel remains the private-equity-to-insurance linkage. Industry estimates indicate insurers now account for 23.4% of private credit capital providers, representing between $0.9 trillion and $1.1 trillion of investment exposure. The US Treasury Department has assembled a team to assess insurer exposure, while the IMF has warned that insurers holding leveraged private credit instruments could face larger-than-expected losses during periods of stress. CAIA data indicates that 6.4% of private credit loans carried deferred-interest, or "bad PIK," structures in early 2026; and more than one-tenth of loans held by private credit funds had been marked down by at least 50%, according to MSCI data.

---

### Why it is under-covered

It is private by design. There are no daily prices, no public ratings on most borrowers, and net asset values are reported with a 60–90 day lag using model-based marks. Opacity is the product feature that makes the risk hard to see — and hard to report. Private credit remained under scrutiny during the third quarter as investors continued to focus on default risk, older loan vintages, software-sector exposure, and BDC liquidity — yet individual fund-level detail still arrives slowly and inconsistently, and there is no single regulatory body with a consolidated view across jurisdictions.

---

### The debate

**The warning case:** default rates are at a record and rising for four consecutive quarters; the instruments doing most of the masking — PIK arrangements, stressed maturity extensions, and interest deferrals — accounted for the majority of Q3 default events, not outright missed payments; gates are now routine at funds holding more than $130 billion in combined assets; a further Fed rate hike is plausible; and insurer balance sheets carry exposures that regulators are only now beginning to quantify.

**The reassurance case (fund managers, some regulators):** fund-level leverage is modest — roughly 1–1.5x, versus 30–40x in pre-2008 securitisation structures; capital is locked up for years rather than runnable in the way bank deposits are; the Federal Reserve's own May 2026 Financial Stability Report called private credit risks "limited and manageable"; and credit fundamentals across several large funds remain largely stable, with some non-accruals falling quarter-over-quarter.

**Both sides accept:** defaults are now at a record, valuation practices are opaque, the sector is untested through a full prolonged downturn, and redemption gates — while functioning as designed — are preventing investors from accessing capital on the timescales they expected.

---

### Stress test (the benign case)

The structural differences from 2008 are real: long lock-ups prevent depositor-style runs, leverage is far lower, and there is no comparable web of CDOs amplifying losses through the system. The most probable adverse path therefore remains not a sudden collapse but a slow shakeout — rising defaults, forced markdowns, restricted redemptions, tighter credit for mid-sized firms — painful for investors and borrowers but, on current evidence, absorbable by the banking system. Banks hold roughly $95 billion in direct credit exposure to private credit funds and BDCs; even under a severe shock scenario, the Fed's 2025 stress tests showed bank capital ratios holding at an 11.8% minimum. The risk to that benign read has, however, become more concrete: persistently high real interest rates are maintaining pressure on highly leveraged borrowers' debt-service capacity and refinancing options, and a further rate increase — projected by twelve of eighteen FOMC members as of September — would add to that pressure before any relief.

---

### Historical parallel

The unavoidable comparison is subprime — opaque, rapidly grown, model-valued credit — but the analogy is contested and imperfect given the leverage and liquidity differences. A closer fit may be the slow-burn savings-and-loan and leveraged-loan workouts of the late 1980s and early 1990s: not a single detonation, but a multi-year grind of markdowns and restructurings that traps capital and tightens lending well after the headlines fade. Issuers with weak or declining earnings, elevated leverage, and approaching maturities have contributed to increased liability-management exercises, restructurings, and defaults — with refinancing risk continuing to increase as maturities approach for loans originated in 2021–22, when base rates were lower and underwriting assumptions were more aggressive.

---

::: box
**In plain terms**

Big investment firms have built a roughly $1.5–$2 trillion business lending directly to mid-sized companies — outside the banks and outside daily market prices. The headline risk is now visible in the data: defaults have hit a record for four quarters running, and at several large funds investors who asked to withdraw their money are getting back only half of what they requested, quarter after quarter. A fresh interest-rate rise in September 2026 adds cost pressure to borrowers already struggling. The losses are concentrated in manufacturing, healthcare, and consumer businesses — not tech, as many expected. Because a lot of this money sits inside insurance and retirement products, ordinary people are exposed without necessarily realising it, and the slow pace of reporting means they may be the last to find out.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Check your exposure.** Ask whether your retirement or insurance products hold 'private credit,' 'direct lending,' or 'alternatives,' and read the fund factsheet carefully.

**Be sceptical of 'high steady yield, no volatility.'** Smooth returns in these funds often mean the price simply is not being updated — not that there is no risk. A gate means your money may be locked in for longer than you planned.

**Understand the lock-up before you commit.** Many of these funds can restrict redemptions to 5% per quarter; if 10% or more of investors want out, you may wait several quarters to recover your capital.

**Diversify.** Do not let one yield-chasing product — however well it performed in recent years — become a large share of your savings. The current environment tests exactly the assumptions on which those returns were built.

**Hold a cash buffer.** If private credit or other illiquid alternatives are part of your portfolio, ensure you have sufficient liquid savings elsewhere so that a gate does not force you into a bad decision elsewhere.
:::

---

### What to watch

- **The Fitch PCDR in Q4 2026:** whether it breaches 6.3% or begins to plateau is the single clearest quantitative signal of whether the slow shakeout is stabilising or accelerating.
- **Redemption gate data for Q4 2026:** specifically whether the funds that prorated for three consecutive quarters begin to clear the backlog — or whether the queue of unfulfilled requests grows further into year-end.
- **Further Fed rate decisions:** twelve of eighteen FOMC members projected at least one more hike before year-end as of the September meeting; each additional 25 basis points directly raises the floating-rate cost burden on the most stressed borrowers.
- **Insurer and pension disclosures on private credit concentration:** the channel into retirement savings remains the least transparent part of the system, and the US Treasury team assessing this exposure has not yet published findings.

## 4. Stablecoins quietly become a pillar — and a fault line — of Treasury demand {#s7_stablecoins}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone who uses crypto or dollar ‘stablecoins’ — and, indirectly, all savers, because these tokens are becoming a real buyer of US government debt.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 4/10 — Moderate** *Fast-growing but still small*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```


**Since the last edition:** The OCC issued its final GENIUS Act implementing rule on 21 October 2026 (effective 18 January 2027), becoming the first federal payment-stablecoin regulator to finalise rules; the Federal Reserve and FDIC remain at proposal stage. Total stablecoin supply eased to roughly $299bn and peg deviations remain negligible, leaving the threat level unchanged.

**Why this level:** A new and run-prone marginal buyer of T-bills, but still modest relative to the system and freshly regulated under the GENIUS Act. **Raises it:** A major stablecoin losing its peg under stress; rapid, thinly-backed growth; a run forcing T-bill fire-sales. **Lowers it:** Smooth GENIUS implementation; full, transparent reserve backing; concentration in well-capitalised issuers.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Stablecoins, almost all of them dollar-pegged, grew to roughly \$300bn outstanding by early 2026 (total supply peaked at about \$310bn in late 2025 before easing to around \$299bn by November 2026 [Established]), from about \$25bn in 2020. The July 2025 GENIUS Act gave them a federal framework requiring 1:1 backing in cash and short-dated Treasuries (93 days or less) plus repo, with monthly disclosure. The US Treasury Secretary has openly framed the law as a tool to cement dollar dominance and “surge” demand for US Treasuries, projecting a multitrillion-dollar market.

**\[Established\]** That makes stablecoin issuers a fast-growing, price-insensitive buyer of Treasury bills — holdings that already rival some sovereign holders. ECB and BIS research finds a \$3.5bn inflow into dollar stablecoins lowers 3-month bill yields by roughly 2.5–3.5 basis points, an effect that more than doubles when bills are scarce. The ECB explicitly describes a “global safe-asset channel” and frets that Europe must field euro stablecoins or cede ground.

### Why it is under-covered

It is filed under “crypto,” which causes serious macro readers to skip it. But the relevant story is not speculation — it is market structure: a new, lightly understood, run-prone intermediary is becoming a material marginal buyer at the short end of the world’s most important bond market.

### The debate

**Boosters argue:** stablecoins extend dollar reach globally, deepen Treasury demand, and modernise payments (instant, programmable, 24/7).

**Skeptics argue:** they import run risk into the Treasury market, lack 24/7 redemption infrastructure, raise illicit-finance questions, and the fiscal benefit is partly offset by lost seigniorage as currency demand falls.

**Both sides accept:** the sector is growing fast, is now macro-relevant to short-end Treasury demand, and concentrates reserves in a narrow band of assets.

### Stress test (the benign case)

GENIUS’s reserve, disclosure and audit requirements are precisely designed to make these instruments boring and run-resistant; full reserve backing in T-bills is far safer than the assets behind past failures. If the rules hold and a few well-capitalised issuers dominate, stablecoins could be a stabilising, structural source of bill demand. The vulnerability: a loss of peg under stress (USD Coin briefly fell to about \$0.87 in March 2023) could force rapid, synchronised T-bill sales into a stressed market — transmitting a crypto shock into Treasury funding, the very plumbing Stories 1 and 2 also run through.

### Historical parallel

The closest analogue is the 2008 “breaking the buck” of the Reserve Primary money-market fund, which triggered a run across prime money funds and a government backstop. Stablecoins are economically similar — fixed-value claims backed by short paper, vulnerable to redemption runs — but without the decades of money-fund regulation and now wired into round-the-clock crypto markets that never close for a cooling-off period.

::: box
**In plain terms**

‘Stablecoins’ are crypto tokens meant to always be worth $1, backed by cash and short-term US government debt. They’ve grown fast enough to matter to the Treasury market. The risk: if a big stablecoin ‘breaks the buck’ in a panic (one briefly did in 2023), it could be forced to dump those Treasuries quickly, pushing a crypto shock into the wider financial plumbing. New US rules (the GENIUS Act) aim to make them safer.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Remember they’re not bank deposits.** Stablecoins aren’t FDIC-insured, and the ‘$1’ peg can break under stress.

**Prefer large, transparent, fully-reserved issuers** that publish audits — and don’t park money you can’t afford to lose in them for a little extra yield.

**Don’t treat the yield as ‘risk-free.’** That yield is payment for real risks.

**For most people this is awareness;** if you don’t use crypto, your exposure is indirect (its effect on the Treasury market).
:::

### What to watch

- Total stablecoin supply and the share held in T-bills vs repo: the size of the new marginal buyer.

- Any peg deviation in major stablecoins under market stress — the run trigger.

- GENIUS implementing rules from Treasury/OCC and the Fed’s stance on payment accounts — the guardrails.

- Concentration: how few issuers dominate, and whether non-financial firms enter via loopholes.

## 5. Clearinghouses: the financial system’s hidden single point of failure {#s21_clearinghouses}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Everyone with savings in markets — a handful of clearinghouses sit at the centre of the financial system, and one failing would be a system-wide event.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 5/10 — Elevated** *Deadline pressure, volatility picking up*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** Since late June 2026, two of the three indicators underpinning this story have moved into a higher band. A sell-off in AI-related stocks pushed the VIX above 22 for the first time in months before easing slightly; the tracker records a reading of 21.46 on 2 November, up from 15.81 at the last edition. The MOVE Index — bond markets' equivalent of the VIX — surged and left equity volatility far behind, with analysts noting it is "making higher lows while the VIX makes lower highs." Higher readings on both gauges raise the probability of a procyclical margin-call episode. On the regulatory front, the SEC took a further step forward on 30 October 2026 by issuing exemptive relief for certain inter-affiliate and non-US repo transactions, resolving one pending question, but the core deadlines remain in place and industry readiness gaps persist — enough to keep friction elevated and the threat level at 5/10 (Elevated) rather than the previous 4/10 (Moderate).

**Why this level:** Clearinghouses concentrate the system's residual risk in a few "too-important-to-fail" hubs, and the US Treasury-clearing mandate will push even more through them just as both equity and bond-market volatility has climbed. **Raises it:** Equity and Treasury volatility both moving to a higher band simultaneously, increasing the probability of procyclical margin-call pressure; the 31 December 2026 cash-clearing deadline arriving with a meaningful share of industry activity still not submitted for clearing. **Lowers it:** Smooth Treasury-clearing implementation with robust capacity; stronger CCP recovery and resolution plans; volatility retreating; the SEC's targeted exemptive relief narrowing some cross-border ambiguity.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** After 2008, regulators pushed vast swaths of trading through central counterparties (CCPs) — clearinghouses that stand between buyer and seller to guarantee trades. This genuinely reduced the tangled, bilateral counterparty risk that amplified the crisis. But it also concentrated the system's residual risk into a handful of "too-important-to-fail" hubs — names like CME, ICE, LCH, Eurex, and DTCC/FICC. A Chicago Fed analysis finds the clearing ecosystem is now *more concentrated than it was in 2007*, before the crisis.

**\[Established\]** The mechanism that makes CCPs safe in normal times can make them dangerous in a panic. CCPs require members to post cash margin that rises automatically with volatility — so their liquidity demands are inherently *procyclical*: exactly when markets are stressed and cash is scarce, the clearinghouse calls for more of it, which can force fire-sales and propagate the stress it was meant to contain. The Fed has flagged this systemic liquidity risk explicitly.

**\[Established\]** The live development is the SEC's mandate to centrally clear US Treasuries — cash transactions by 31 December 2026 and repo by 30 June 2027 — the regulators' fix for the basis-trade risk discussed in Story 1. This will route an enormous additional volume through CCPs, *increasing* concentration. FICC currently clears more than $12 trillion in average daily transactions, with activity levels up 165% since the SEC first proposed the Treasury clearing mandate. In other words, the cure for one chokepoint (hidden leverage in Treasuries) deepens another (clearinghouse concentration).

**\[Established\]** Readiness is uneven. A survey conducted in June 2026 found that 45% of firms still need more regulatory clarity to progress their readiness, and 51% of firms have no formal contingency plan for missed readiness. A July 2026 DTCC survey reported that 79% of responding FICC Government Securities Division Netting Members already had the necessary account setups, but respondents nonetheless estimated that $300–$400 billion of average daily Treasury cash activity was still not being submitted for clearing.

**\[Established\]** On 30 October 2026, the SEC issued targeted exemptive relief (Release No. 34-106789) for certain categories of repo. The order allows a capped amount of otherwise in-scope repo with non-US clients to remain bilateral and expands the inter-affiliate exclusion beyond banks, broker-dealers, and futures commission merchants. The exemptions are narrow rather than a retreat from central clearing; the SEC said the change addresses practical cross-border concerns while preserving the risk-management benefits of clearing. The core deadlines — 31 December 2026 for cash, 30 June 2027 for repo — are unchanged.

**\[Contested\]** Whether the remaining readiness gaps are manageable or material is disputed. DTCC has stated it believes the industry is well positioned for the Treasury cash clearing deadline. Critics note that more than half of surveyed firms lack a contingency plan, and that the December deadline is weeks away.

### Why it is under-covered

CCPs are deliberately invisible plumbing — the system works precisely because no one thinks about them. There is no retail hook and the machinery is arcane (default waterfalls, margin models, mutualised loss funds). Only three CCPs have ever failed (in 1974, 1983, and 1987), which breeds a complacency that the consequences of a fourth would shatter. The Treasury-clearing story is covered in trade publications but rarely framed for general audiences as a clearinghouse concentration question.

### The debate

**The reassurance camp argues:** CCPs work — they survived 2008, 2020, and 2022 — with deep, pre-funded default buffers, strict margining, and far more transparency than the bilateral web they replaced; concentration brings standardisation and oversight. On the mandate, the SEC and DTCC have signalled confidence in readiness, targeted exemptive relief has addressed key cross-border friction points, and cleared volumes are already rising substantially ahead of the deadline.

**The concern camp argues:** concentration plus procyclical margin calls turns CCPs into potential transmission mechanisms; their risk is poorly understood; recovery and resolution plans are untested; and the Treasury mandate is loading still more onto them just as equity and bond volatility have both moved higher. The gap between firms that have completed preparations and those still without contingency plans is uncomfortably wide with the December deadline imminent.

**Both sides accept:** central clearing reduced bilateral counterparty risk but concentrated residual risk; margin demands are procyclical; and the key CCPs are now critical infrastructure whose failure would be a system-wide event.

### Stress test (the benign case)

The buffers are real and large: pre-funded default funds, layered "waterfalls," conservative margining, and stress tests designed around multiple simultaneous member defaults. CCPs absorbed the 2020 dash-for-cash and the 2022 commodity and gilt shocks without failing. The vulnerability is the tail no buffer is sized for: a fast, correlated volatility spike that triggers margin calls system-wide — as the UK's 2022 LDI episode and the 2022 nickel-market chaos foreshadowed — or the failure of a large clearing member, against a backdrop of rising concentration and a mandatory deadline that forces volumes through a single infrastructure node on a fixed date.

### Historical parallel

The clearest warnings are recent and partial: the UK's September 2022 pension (LDI) crisis, where margin calls on leveraged hedges forced a doom-loop of gilt selling until the Bank of England intervened; and the March 2020 "dash for cash," when margin demands drained liquidity system-wide. The 1987 crash nearly broke the clearing system and did topple the Hong Kong Futures Exchange's clearer. The pattern: clearing is invisible and reassuring until a volatility spike turns its safety mechanism into an accelerant. Analysts have noted that the MOVE Index "flashed turbulence before the VIX in 2022, in 2023," suggesting bond-market stress tends to lead equity stress — a sequence that would put CCP margin calls at the front of any future cascade.

::: box
**In plain terms**

After the 2008 crisis, regulators routed most trading through a few "clearinghouses" — middlemen that sit between buyers and sellers and guarantee trades. This made the system safer in normal times, but it piled the leftover risk into a handful of giant hubs that are now too important to fail. The catch: these hubs demand more cash from their members exactly when markets are panicking and cash is scarce — which can make a crisis worse. A new rule pushing US government-bond trading through them (the fix for a problem in Story 1) is concentrating even more risk in one place, with the first big deadline arriving on 31 December 2026 and a meaningful slice of the industry still without a contingency plan. On top of that, both equity and bond-market volatility have risen since mid-year. Failures are very rare and the safety buffers are big, so this remains a low-probability tail risk — but if it ever broke, it would be a system-wide event.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**This is awareness — there is no direct retail action,** but it is part of why broad diversification and a cash buffer matter: in a true plumbing crisis, everything sells off together for a while.

**Keep an emergency fund outside the markets.** The recurring lesson of every plumbing scare (2008, 2020, 2022) is that cash on hand means you are never forced to sell at the bottom.

**Be sceptical of leverage and "can't-lose" hedges.** The 2022 UK pension blow-up came from leveraged strategies that needed cash exactly when it had vanished; the same logic applies to your own finances — avoid being a forced seller.

**Watch your statements, not the headlines.** The risk here lives in plumbing most people never see; what matters to you is whether your own portfolio is resilient enough to ride out a period when everything falls together.

**Don't lose sleep over this one specifically.** It is a low-probability tail risk handled by people whose job is to handle it; the general "cash buffer and diversify" habit is the right response.
:::

### What to watch

- Market volatility (VIX and MOVE) and margin-call episodes — both gauges have already risen since the last edition, and a further correlated spike is the primary trigger for procyclical CCP stress.

- US Treasury-clearing cash deadline (31 December 2026): whether the remaining $300–$400 billion of daily activity not yet submitted for clearing migrates smoothly, and whether the minority of firms without contingency plans causes disruption.

- US Treasury-clearing repo deadline (30 June 2027) and whether implementation friction for cross-border and inter-affiliate transactions eases following the October 2026 exemptive relief.

- Any large clearing-member distress — the most direct single-entity failure path — and the results of CCP stress tests and recovery/resolution planning from regulators.

## 6. The AI build-out’s circular money machine {#s8_ai_capex}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone with a pension, 401(k), or index fund — because a handful of AI names now dominate the market you are probably invested in.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 7/10 — High** *Large, debt-fuelled, concentrated*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#E8710A"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** Q3 2026 earnings (reported 28–29 October) confirmed that hyperscaler capex continues to far outpace AI revenue: Azure grew 48% year-on-year while Microsoft's quarterly capex hit $39.4 bn (~+100% YoY); Google Cloud grew 103% while Alphabet's capex reached $51.4 bn (+114% YoY); AWS grew 39% to $46.0 bn in its sixth consecutive quarter of acceleration, with Amazon's full-year capex guide approaching $220 bn. Meanwhile, GPU-backed debt stress escalated: CoreWeave's five-year CDS spread hit an intraday record of 1,194.5 bps on 15 October (S&P Global Market Intelligence via CNBC), though no payment default has been recorded. The threat level is unchanged.

**Why this level:** Enormous, increasingly debt-financed spending concentrated in a few index-dominating names, with a 2026–27 refinancing wall — though hyperscaler cash flows are strong. **Raises it:** An AI-revenue disappointment; widening bond spreads on the big issuers; GPU-debt refinancing stress. **Lowers it:** AI revenue ramping to meet capex; build-out funded from cash flow; clearer, consistent disclosure.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Spending on AI data centres has reached a scale without precedent. Big Tech’s 2026 capital expenditure is projected to exceed \$600bn — up roughly 50% after a ~60% jump in 2025 — with several estimates of the broader AI build-out around \$650–700bn for the year. Capital intensity (capex as a share of revenue) has climbed to levels that are historically unheard of for the sector.

**\[Established\]** Two features make this more than a spending story. First, **circularity**: the same companies invest in, lend to, and buy from one another — chipmakers funding model labs that commit to cloud providers that buy those chips. Supporters call it a virtuous circle that locks in scarce supply; critics call it round-tripping that can inflate apparent demand. Second, **concentration**: the combined weight of Meta, Alphabet, Amazon and Oracle in the US investment-grade corporate bond index nearly doubled over the year to April 2026, from 2.2% to 4.1%, as they issued debt to fund the build-out; by October 2026 the US investment-grade OAS (a proxy for big-issuer spreads) had widened to around 0.94%, up from 0.75% in June **[Established]**.

**\[Contested\]** Estimates of the debt involved vary; one widely cited figure puts buildout-related debt around \$1.5tn over time. **\[Forecast\]** Consultancy work cited in academic analysis flags a potential ~\$800bn-a-year gap by 2030 between committed infrastructure spending and realised AI revenue. The first wave of debt backed by GPUs (the chips themselves) matures in 2026–2027, just as those chips depreciate.

### Why it is under-covered

The headline — “AI is booming” — is everywhere. The financing plumbing underneath is not: vendor financing, take-or-pay contracts, off-balance-sheet special-purpose vehicles, and supplier financing showing up as rising accounts payable. Disclosure is inconsistent across companies, which is exactly what makes the aggregate risk hard to see.

### The debate

**Bulls argue:** demand for computing is real and exploding; locking in suppliers and customers through paired investment-and-purchase deals is rational in a supply-constrained market (Janus Henderson’s “virtuous circle”; KKR’s “beyond the bubble”). Hyperscalers are highly profitable and investment-grade.

**Skeptics argue:** circular deals can make demand look bigger than it is; the gap between capex and actual AI revenue is large and widening (Q3 2026 results show Google Cloud revenue of \$30.7bn versus capex of \$51.4bn in a single quarter **[Established]**); GPU-backed debt plus rapid chip depreciation is a refinancing trap; and concentration means a stumble hits the whole index.

**Both sides accept:** the spending is real and enormous, it is increasingly debt-financed, and it is concentrated in a handful of firms whose fortunes now move the broad market.

### Stress test (the benign case)

If AI adoption inflects upward as the bulls expect, today’s capex looks visionary rather than reckless: the hyperscalers genuinely can fund much of this from cash flow, their credit is strong, and demand fills the capacity. The weak point in that case is timing and correlation — revenue must ramp before depreciation and debt maturities bite, and because the same few names dominate both the bond and equity indices, a single disappointment transmits everywhere at once. A Moody’s scenario of a sharp decline maps contagion into banks via construction loans and GPU-backed facilities.

### Historical parallel

The closest analogue is the 2000–02 telecom build-out, where vendor financing (equipment makers lending customers the money to buy their gear) masked weak end-demand until it collapsed. Academic work also draws on 2008 (counterparty opacity and off-balance-sheet leverage) and the 2014–16 oil bust (physical overbuilding meeting a price collapse). The recurring lesson: infrastructure booms funded by circular, debt-laden financing tend to overshoot, and the bill arrives when financing tightens.

::: box
**In plain terms**

The companies building artificial intelligence are spending staggering sums — over $600 billion this year, with Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft alone guiding to a combined capex well above $450 billion for 2026 **[Established]** — much of it borrowed. A worry is that they keep investing in and buying from each other, which can make demand look bigger and healthier than it really is. Because just a few giant companies are doing most of this, they now make up an unusually large slice of the stock and bond markets. If AI doesn’t earn money as fast as hoped, the disappointment could hit not just those companies but the index funds most people’s retirement savings sit in.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Know what you actually own.** A standard S&P 500 index fund is now heavily weighted toward a few AI-linked giants. “Diversified” may be less diversified than you think — look up your fund’s top-10 holdings and their combined weight.

**Diversify deliberately.** Spreading across regions, sectors, and asset types (not just more US tech) reduces how much one AI stumble can hurt you.

**Avoid concentrating in single AI names** or chasing the hype with money you can’t afford to lose; resist leverage and “story” bets.

**Keep an emergency cash buffer.** Bubble timing is unknowable; cash on hand means a market drop doesn’t force you to sell at the bottom.
:::

### What to watch

- Hyperscaler free cash flow vs capex — the moment build-out is funded by debt rather than profits.

- AI revenue disclosures vs the capex–revenue gap; signs of under-used (“idle”) capacity.

- Corporate bond spreads on the big four issuers, and any credit-rating pressure.

- GPU-backed debt maturities in 2026–2027 and refinancing terms; CoreWeave CDS spreads and bond yields as a real-time gauge of GPU-debt stress.

## 7. The commercial real-estate “maturity wall” and your regional bank {#s9_cre}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone who banks with a regional or community bank, works in a downtown economy, or owns property near struggling office districts.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 6/10 — Elevated** *Active distress, wall now extended*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** The CMBS office delinquency rate, which stood at its then-record of 12.34% in January 2026, pulled back briefly before climbing to 12.16% in September 2026 per Trepp — crossing into a higher stress band in the tracker's methodology. Separately, the pace of US bank failures has accelerated sharply: six banks failed in 2026 by late September, the most of any year this decade, with California's Nano Banc — the largest of the six at $736m in assets and explicitly linked to CRE enforcement actions — closing on 25 September. Crucially, the maturity wall is now understood to extend well beyond 2026, with S&P Global Market Intelligence estimating a fresh peak of approximately $1.26tn in maturities in 2027, meaning the pressure is not fading but shifting forward.

**Why this level:** Record-high CMBS office delinquencies, a maturity wall that is rolling into 2027 rather than clearing, and the highest count of bank failures in a decade together push the threat above the prior edition's reading. **Raises it:** The CMBS office delinquency rate has crossed a new stress threshold at 12.16%; six bank failures in 2026 — three times the pace of 2024 and 2025 — include at least one explicitly CRE-linked institution; the 2027 maturity peak is now projected to be larger than 2026's. **Lowers it:** The six failed banks collectively held only roughly $1.4bn in assets; regional bank stress remains in the "isolated" rather than "cluster" band; private credit continues to act as a partial relief valve; insured depositors were protected in every 2026 failure.

---

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** The Mortgage Bankers Association reported that $875bn in commercial and multifamily mortgage debt — approximately 17% of the $5tn outstanding — is scheduled to mature in 2026. Much of it was written in 2019–2022 at 3–4% rates and is maturing into a markedly higher-rate environment, against property values that are in many cases 16–35% lower. The 2026 total represents a 9% decline from the $957bn that matured in 2025, suggesting the peak of the maturity wave is beginning to pass — but the volume that remains requires active management.

**\[Established\]** The pressure is not, however, simply clearing. S&P Global Market Intelligence estimates that approximately $1.26tn of US CRE mortgages will mature in 2027, the peak of the current maturity cycle. The wall has in effect been extended: extensions and shorter-term loans are pushing maturities into 2027 and beyond rather than resolving them.

**\[Established\]** Office remains the epicentre. The Trepp CMBS office delinquency rate rose to 11.91% in July 2026, then the CMBS office delinquency rate hit 12.16% in September 2026, according to Trepp — remaining close to the all-time record set at 12.34% in January. At prevailing refinancing rates, a majority of maturing office loans would fail a 1.0x debt-service-coverage-ratio test. Banks hold roughly $1.89tn of CRE loans on income-producing property, with securitised lenders holding another ~$753bn.

**\[Established\]** The regional-bank dimension has sharpened. Irvine, California-based Nano Banc failed on 25 September, meaning 2026 is outpacing every other year this decade in terms of bank collapses, with six. The six held only about $1.4bn in combined assets and nearly all of them had been operating under regulatory enforcement orders for years before closing. In recent years, Nano Banc had been the subject of enforcement actions in connection with both commercial real estate lending and governance issues. The FDIC estimates Nano's failure will cost the Deposit Insurance Fund roughly $114m.

**\[Single-source\]** One industry analysis estimates regional banks hold about $396bn of the maturing debt and that over half exceed the 300% CRE-to-capital level that regulators watch. **\[Established\]** Regulators continue to tighten oversight of CRE-heavy banks, and the Fed's loan-officer survey shows banks have tightened CRE standards for multiple consecutive quarters.

---

### Why it is under-covered

"Office buildings in trouble" sounds like a niche real-estate problem. The under-covered angle is the link to regional banks — which hold a disproportionate share of this debt — and therefore to local lending, small-business credit, and the deposits of ordinary people who never think about CMBS. Also under-reported: the 2026 maturity wall was widely framed as the climax of the cycle, but extensions mean the true peak is now shifting to 2027, so the problem has a longer tail than most headlines suggest.

---

### The debate

**The alarmist case:** a slow-motion solvency drain on regional banks as extensions expire, values stay depressed, and losses crystallise. The fact that 2026 is running at three times the bank-failure pace of 2024 and 2025 — and that the 2027 maturity volume now looks larger than 2026's — suggests the grind has longer to run than the market expected.

**The reassuring case:** this wall is the most widely anticipated in history, so markets and banks have been provisioning and adapting (extensions, recapitalisations, private-credit takeouts); the six 2026 failures collectively held barely $1.4bn in assets; CRE loans carry less leverage and more cushion than 2008-era mortgages; and bank CRE delinquencies remain far below the ~9% peak of 2010. Rather than a crisis, the maturity wall has evolved into a prolonged challenge, with borrowers and lenders increasingly relying on extensions and shorter-term loans, pushing maturities further into the future.

**Both sides accept:** office values have fallen hard, a large slug of debt must be refinanced into much higher rates, regional banks are the concentrated pressure point, and the resolution timeline now extends clearly into 2027 and beyond.

---

### Stress test (the benign case)

Because everyone sees this coming, it may resolve as a grind rather than a crash: well-located properties with strong tenants refinance adequately, private credit fills gaps, and losses are absorbed gradually by well-capitalised lenders. Insured depositors are protected regardless — every 2026 bank failure was resolved with deposits transferred to an acquiring bank. The vulnerability is concentration and confidence: a handful of regional banks with heavy office exposure and thin capital could still fail, and — as 2023 showed — fear can move faster than fundamentals once a name is in the spotlight. The rolling of maturities into 2027 extends that window of vulnerability.

---

### Historical parallel

The early-1990s savings-and-loan and CRE bust is the template for a drawn-out workout that takes years and shutters weak lenders without sinking the system. 2008 is the scarier comparison, but most analysts argue CRE today has lower leverage, more diverse funding, and far lower delinquencies than residential mortgages did then — a slow bleed, not a sudden break. The current episode more closely resembles the S&L aftermath: a predictable, multi-year resolution process in which the weakest, most governance-troubled institutions fail first, depositors are made whole, and the broader system absorbs losses gradually.

---

::: box
**In plain terms**

Lots of loans on office buildings and other commercial property were taken out when interest rates were very low. They are now coming due all at once — and not just in 2026 but increasingly in 2027 too, because many lenders have simply pushed the problem forward by extending loans rather than resolving them. Rates are much higher now, and many of those buildings are worth far less, especially offices. Smaller "regional" banks made a lot of these loans, so the strain lands on them. Six US banks failed in 2026 — more than any other year this decade — and the most recent, California's Nano Banc, had a history of CRE-related enforcement actions. Your insured deposits are safe (every 2026 failure was resolved with deposits transferred to another bank), but if local banks are nursing property losses, they lend less, which can squeeze small businesses and local economies. It is more likely to be a slow grind than a sudden crash, partly because everyone has seen it coming — but the grind now has a longer runway than most people expected.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Stay within deposit-insurance limits.** In the US, FDIC insurance covers $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. If you are near that threshold at one institution, spreading deposits across banks (or using insured-sweep options) keeps you fully covered regardless of what happens to any single bank.

**Know your bank.** You do not need to panic-switch, but it is reasonable to be aware if your bank is unusually concentrated in commercial real estate; bank financial summaries are public and freely available from the FDIC.

**Keep some liquidity outside any single institution** so that an isolated bank problem never freezes your access to cash when you need it.

**Avoid leverage on illiquid assets.** The current environment — higher-for-longer rates, compressed property values — penalises borrowers who cannot service debt if valuations slip further.

**If you own affected property,** engage lenders early on extensions or restructuring; the worst outcomes consistently hit those who wait until maturity day to start that conversation.
:::

---

### What to watch

- **CMBS office delinquency and special-servicing rates** — the September 2026 Trepp reading of 12.16% is the clearest live distress gauge; watch whether it pushes through the January 2026 record of 12.34%.
- **Regional-bank loan-loss provisions, CRE concentration ratios, and any rating downgrades** — especially for banks operating under existing enforcement orders.
- **The 2027 maturity pipeline** — S&P Global Market Intelligence now estimates $1.26tn maturing in 2027; how much of that is genuinely refinanced versus re-extended will determine whether the wall clears or simply shifts again.
- **Private-credit appetite to absorb maturing CRE** — the key relief valve; any tightening in private-credit supply would accelerate forced resolutions.

## 8. The hidden consumer-debt squeeze: subprime cars and “phantom” instalments {#s11_consumer_debt}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone with a car loan, anyone who uses Buy Now Pay Later, parents of Gen Z, and anyone applying for a mortgage near someone who does.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 6/10 — Elevated** *Acute at the bottom, contained overall*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** The subprime-auto stress signal has been confirmed through September 2026: KBRA's non-prime auto ABS 60-plus-day delinquency rate eased slightly to 6.64% (up 25 basis points month-on-month from August but below the January 2026 peak), while BNPL delinquency trends held steady. No indicator changed band; the threat level is unchanged.

**Why this level:** Record subprime-auto stress and invisible BNPL debt at the fragile end, but small relative to the system with no clear broad spillover yet. **Raises it:** Rising unemployment; BNPL/auto delinquencies bleeding into cards and mortgages; a student-loan default surge. **Lowers it:** Stable employment and real wages; BNPL data reaching the credit bureaus; delinquencies plateauing.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Two pockets of American household stress are flashing red while the averages look calm. First, subprime auto: 60-plus-day delinquencies among subprime borrowers hit their highest level in 32 years in January 2026 (Fitch: 6.90%), and while the rate has since eased — KBRA's non-prime ABS index stood at 6.64% in September 2026 — repossessions rose 43% between 2022 and 2024, and loan terms have stretched dramatically. [Established] Loan terms have stretched sharply: more than 30% of subprime loans originated in the first half of 2026 are scheduled to remain outstanding until the financed vehicles are over 12 years old, compared with just 7% at end-2019. Negative equity (owing more than the car is worth) remains at records. [Single-source]

**\[Established\]** Second, “Buy Now, Pay Later” (BNPL) — instalment plans from Klarna, Affirm, Afterpay and others — which regulators (the Richmond Fed) literally call “phantom debt” because much of it isn’t reported to the main credit bureaus. A borrower can stack loans across several apps and still look debt-free on a credit report. Because BNPL balances are small, people prioritise paying them — so their larger debts (cards, car, student loans) default first, meaning official metrics understate the strain.

**\[Contested\]** BNPL’s size is genuinely murky and reported numbers conflict. The Richmond Fed estimates real BNPL transaction value around \$70bn in 2025 (~1.1% of card spending) — small. Other sources cite annual purchase volume (GMV) near \$560bn and outstanding balances above \$400bn. These measure different things (a year’s spending vs money currently owed), which is exactly why the risk is hard to size. **\[Single-source\]** Separately, one analysis reports US student-loan defaults reappearing sharply — around a million in late 2025 and 2.6 million in early 2026 — as pandemic-era forbearance ends.

### Why it is under-covered

Aggregate consumer numbers look fine — overall credit-card delinquency is stable, real incomes edged up. That average masks a sharply “K-shaped” reality where the bottom tier is in deep stress, and a whole category of borrowing (BNPL) is partly invisible to the data everyone relies on. As one investor put it, there are two possible bubbles right now and only the AI one gets the headlines.

### The debate

**The alarmists argue:** this is the subprime-mortgage playbook — risky consumer debt, sliced up and partly invisible — with record auto delinquencies and hidden BNPL stacking pointing to a coming reckoning.

**The Fed and regulators counter:** BNPL is still small relative to credit cards; much of the “record” subprime auto delinquency reflects credit-score migration (during the pandemic, many borrowers’ scores rose, shrinking the subprime pool and flattering its delinquency math); and there’s no clear evidence yet of broad systemic spillover.

**Both sides accept:** the most financially fragile households are genuinely strained, BNPL data gaps are real and growing, and these are early-warning lights even if not yet a system-wide fire.

### Stress test (the benign case)

The reassuring read is solid: BNPL charge-off rates have actually been low (under 2% in recent reporting), credit-card delinquencies are near pre-pandemic norms, real median wages rose faster than inflation in early 2026, and the subprime-auto “record” is partly a statistical artifact. The risk to that case is the hidden layer — if rising unemployment meets phantom debt and the end of student-loan forbearance, stress can accelerate quickly and show up in the larger loans first, where it does real damage.

### Historical parallel

2008 subprime is the cautionary tale invoked most — invisible, fragmented, under-measured risk — though the scale here is far smaller and the systemic plumbing different. A fairer frame is the “K-shaped” consumer of recent cycles: averages hide a widening gap, and the people at the bottom run out of buffers long before the headline data admits it.

::: box
**In plain terms**

On paper, American consumers look okay. But beneath the average, the people with the weakest finances are struggling: car-loan delinquencies among shaky borrowers hit a 32-year high in January 2026 and remain elevated — 6.64% of non-prime borrowers were 60-plus days late in September 2026, and repossessions have jumped. At the same time, “pay in 4” instalment apps have let people pile up small debts that mostly don’t show on credit reports — so lenders (and the official statistics) can’t see them. People keep these small instalments current and let bigger bills slide, which hides how stretched they are. It’s not a national crisis yet, but it’s a warning light — and it’s easy to fall into personally without noticing.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Track every BNPL plan you have** in one place (a note or spreadsheet). The danger of “pay in 4” is that four small plans across four apps add up to a payment you can’t make.

**Treat BNPL as debt, not a discount.** If you couldn’t buy it with cash or on a card you’ll clear this month, pause before splitting it into instalments.

**Don’t roll negative equity into a new car loan.** Wrapping what you still owe on an old car into a new, longer loan is how people get deeply “underwater.” Keep cars longer; aim for loans of 60 months or less.

**Build a small emergency fund first** (even $500–$1,000). It’s the buffer that stops a surprise bill from becoming a late payment or a new instalment plan.

**If you have federal student loans,** confirm your repayment status now that forbearance has ended — don’t let a missed payment quietly become a default on your credit file.
:::

### What to watch

- Affirm/Klarna delinquency and charge-off disclosures — the clearest window into the invisible layer.

- Whether credit-card and auto delinquencies rise even as BNPL absorbs spending — a sign underlying stress is worse than any single metric.

- Progress on BNPL data flowing to credit bureaus (FICO’s BNPL-inclusive scores).

- Student-loan default figures and the unemployment rate — the accelerants.

## 9. The quiet retreat of insurance — and why it threatens your home’s value {#s13_insurance}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Every homeowner and renter — especially in disaster-exposed areas — plus anyone with a mortgage or hoping to buy.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 5/10 — Elevated** *Building, with household-level damage now*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** The tracker held at 5/10 (Elevated) through end-October 2026, confirmed by the October 2026 NAIC MCAS data and Aon's Q3 2026 catastrophe recap. Non-renewal rates remain at the 'rising' band; no indicator changed band since the last edition.

**Why this level:** Premiums and non-renewals are rising and the value-and-mortgage channel is real, but the systemic transmission is still gradual. **Raises it:** A major catastrophe season; accelerating insurer exits; home-value declines in high-risk zones. **Lowers it:** Successful mitigation and pricing reforms; reinsurance capacity returning; credible policy backstops.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Property insurers are raising premiums sharply, declining to renew policies, and exiting whole regions as climate-driven disaster losses mount — not just in California and Florida, but in places like New England that aren’t thought of as high-risk. US insured catastrophe losses reached \$112.7bn in 2024, up 36% year-on-year. **[Established]** Through the first nine months of 2026, global insured catastrophe losses reached \$91bn — the lowest nine-month figure since 2019 and well below the five-year average of \$135bn — though no single quarter has produced an event exceeding \$10bn in insured losses. A Consumer Federation analysis found roughly 1 in 13 US homeowners now uninsured, leaving about \$1.6tn in home value unprotected.

**\[Established\]** This is becoming a financial-stability issue, not just a household-budget one. Because mortgage lenders require insurance, when coverage becomes unaffordable or unavailable, home sales fall through, values drop, and — as the Dallas Fed has documented — mortgage delinquencies and defaults rise. A US Senate Budget Committee investigation described the trajectory as “2008 all over again,” except potentially more permanent: a flooded-out or uninsurable home may never recover its value.

**\[Single-source\]** Supporting figures to treat as leads: a 2026 survey found nearly half of home buyers/sellers hit insurance problems and 21% had a deal fall through; in Louisiana, 30–40% of mortgage applications reportedly fail over insurance costs; premiums rose around 20% over two years. **\[Established\]** State “FAIR plans” — insurers of last resort — now operate in 34 states plus DC, and are absorbing more risk as private insurers pull back.

### Why it is under-covered

It’s reported as a personal-finance gripe — “my premium went up” — rather than as a systemic threat to the housing market and the financial system. The mechanism that turns rising premiums into falling home values and mortgage defaults is the under-told part.

### The debate

**The financial-stability camp argues:** this is a slow-building 2008-style risk — uninsurable homes lose value, mortgages sour, and losses ripple through lenders and local tax bases.

**The market-signal camp argues:** rising premiums are climate risk being priced correctly; suppressing prices or over-relying on state insurers of last resort just hides the risk and pushes the cost onto everyone else.

**Both sides accept:** premiums are rising fast, availability is shrinking, more homes are uninsured, and the mortgage market is exposed wherever insurance becomes unavailable.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Insurance markets adapt: new pricing models, parametric products, reinsurance, mitigation incentives, and stronger building codes can restore coverage in many areas, and most of the country is not at the extreme tail of risk. The vulnerability is that the worst-hit areas may be genuinely, permanently uninsurable, and that the damage concentrates on lower-income and minority households least able to absorb it — turning a market adjustment into a localized wealth shock.

### Historical parallel

The comparison everyone reaches for is 2008 — a housing-finance shock building quietly until it cascades. The crucial difference, and why some call it worse, is permanence: 2008 property values eventually rebounded, but a home that can’t be insured against a recurring disaster may not. Observers also note the map of uninsurable areas can echo historical redlining, concentrating harm on the same communities.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Insuring a home is getting much more expensive, and in some places you can’t get insurance at all, because disasters are causing record losses. This matters beyond your bill: you generally can’t get a mortgage without insurance, so when coverage disappears, buyers vanish, home values fall, and more people miss mortgage payments. Experts warn this could hit the housing market the way the 2008 crisis did — but possibly more lastingly, because an uninsurable home may never regain its value. It’s spreading beyond the obvious places like Florida and California.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Don’t go uninsured to save money.** An uninsured disaster can wipe out your largest asset. If premiums are painful, first compare quotes widely — prices vary a lot between insurers.

**Raise your deductible thoughtfully** to lower premiums if (and only if) you have the savings to cover that deductible after a loss.

**Invest in mitigation — it can cut premiums and keep you insurable.** Roof upgrades, defensible space against wildfire, storm shutters, and water-leak sensors are often rewarded by insurers and sometimes subsidised by state programs.

**Document your home now.** Photos/video of every room and major possession, stored in the cloud, make claims far easier and fairer after a disaster.

**Before buying a home,** get an insurance quote before you commit — not after — and check the area’s disaster history and whether insurers are still writing new policies there. Know whether a state FAIR plan is your only option.

**Review your coverage limits** against today’s rebuilding costs; many policies are under-insured after years of construction inflation.
:::

### What to watch

- Insurer non-renewal and market-exit announcements by state; growth in FAIR-plan enrollment (NAIC MCAS data show company-initiated non-renewal rates rose 96–216% across all four US regions between 2018 and 2024).

- The Dallas Fed / academic data linking insurance costs to mortgage delinquencies (single-family mortgage delinquency remained at 1.86% as of Q1 2026, a benign reading, but the structural link to insurance availability persists).

- Home-value softening in high-risk zip codes — the early sign of the value channel biting.

- Federal or state policy responses (backstops, mitigation funding, disclosure rules).

## 10. The silent debt crisis in the developing world {#s4_dev_debt}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Mainly people in developing countries (through austerity and lost services); for everyone else it's a moral, migration, and geopolitical story — and a risk inside emerging-market funds.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 5/10 — Elevated** *High human toll, lower systemic spillover*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** Since June 2026, the stress indicator has moved up and then partially back. The IMF programme count softened to "calm" as of October, helped by a $1.9 billion Extended Fund Facility approved for Bolivia in early October — that 36-month arrangement, which reached a staff-level agreement in July and was approved by Bolivia's Congress in September, is Bolivia's first IMF programme since 2006 and is expected to catalyse more than $5 billion in additional financing. Offsetting that relative calm, Senegal became the most prominent new stress case: on 1 September 2026 the Senegalese authorities and the IMF reached a staff-level agreement on a 36-month Extended Credit Facility of about $2.2 billion, under which the country will seek to restructure its public external debt through an enhanced version of the G20 Common Framework. Importantly, the IMF agreement is intended to restore debt sustainability after Senegal uncovered more than $11 billion in previously misreported borrowing. No new sovereign external bond payment has been missed as of 2 November 2026, which is why the indicator eased back from its August–September peak; but the underlying pressures remain firmly in place.

**Why this level:** A genuine, ongoing crisis for billions, but fragmented across many small economies and walled off from rich-world markets, so the global-financial threat is moderate even as the human cost is high. **Raises it:** A strong-dollar surge or commodity shock tipping several countries at once; stalled restructurings and a rising count of countries like Senegal announcing restructuring intent. US 10-year real yields have risen from 2.25% to 2.63% between editions [Established], tightening the external-financing screw. **Lowers it:** No new sovereign payment defaults materialised in the period; dollar index is broadly flat; the Bolivia programme demonstrates that the multilateral system can still close deals.

---

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** As of mid-2026, 75 of 119 low- and middle-income countries assessed by the IMF and rating agencies are in or at risk of debt distress. UN figures put annual developing-country debt servicing near $1.4 trillion, and more than 3.4 billion people — close to half of humanity — live in countries that spend more on debt interest than on health or education. In low-income countries, interest payments now consume more than 15% of fiscal revenues.

**\[Established\]** The period since June 2026 has brought both managed rescues and new fault lines. Bolivia received multilateral support after its deepest economic crisis in roughly four decades: the IMF approved a $1.9 billion financing programme including an immediate $214 million disbursement, designed to help the new government reverse a sharp economic slump, with the board billing it as a path to restore macroeconomic stability while protecting vulnerable households. The new Bolivian president introduced market-oriented reforms including cutting fuel subsidies and adopting a flexible exchange-rate regime after 15 years of a fixed rate, and is also planning to loosen state control over energy and mining to attract foreign investment.

**\[Established\]** Senegal has become Africa's most closely watched debt case. The IMF had halted its previous $1.8 billion programme in 2024 after the incoming administration disclosed far more public debt than its predecessor had reported; IMF staff estimated the extra debt at more than $11 billion based on end-2023 figures, with several analysts placing the total at around $13 billion, roughly a quarter of Senegal's economy. On 28 August 2026, Moody's cut Senegal's issuer ratings from Caa1 to Caa2 with a negative outlook, citing rising refinancing pressures, an interest-to-revenue ratio near 24%, and total 2026 financing needs of around 25% of rebased GDP. A staff-level agreement for a new $2.2 billion programme was reached on 1 September 2026, but as of 2 November 2026 the IMF Executive Board had not yet formally approved it, and bondholders holding about $2.2 billion of Senegal's international bonds have warned Dakar against seeking a haircut on their debt.

**\[Established\]** The broader financing environment has tightened. Sovereign debt sustainability emerged as a major concern at the 2026 IMF–World Bank Annual Meetings, with average 10-year government bond yields across the G7 increasing by more than one percentage point between February and September 2026. While the IMF and multilateral development banks have provided countries with enough to avoid widespread default, they have stopped short of what is needed to restore growth, and a record share of poor countries net-repaid the IMF in 2026 — a drain set to widen by 2030.

**\[Forecast\]** Absent further action, around 40 of the world's poorest countries will face a stark choice: make further sharp cuts to spending at significant cost to growth and human lives, or finance deficits through exorbitant private borrowing likely to lead to eventual default — with both roads leading to crisis.

**\[Established\]** Africa remains the epicentre. Earlier restructurings (Zambia, Ghana) have been slow and partial. The "Turn Debt into Hope" Jubilee campaign has kept calls for broader relief on the political agenda, and leaders continue to argue the credit-ratings system punishes reform rather than rewarding it.

### Why it is under-covered

The UN Secretary-General named the exact reason: because the crisis is concentrated in poor countries, it is "not judged to pose a systemic risk to the global financial system" — so markets, and therefore most financial media, look away. The Senegal episode, arriving with a hidden-debt scandal rather than an obvious macroeconomic collapse, illustrates how these crises can fester unseen until they become acute. It is a humanitarian and geopolitical catastrophe that is invisible precisely because it does not threaten rich-world portfolios directly.

### The debate

**Creditors and the IMF argue:** much of the debt is sustainable after fiscal adjustment; lending and reform programmes are the path back to market access. The Bolivia and (pending) Senegal programmes are offered as evidence the system can deliver.

**Critics argue:** the IMF functions as "the world's debt collector," its prescribed measures (subsidy cuts, VAT increases, spending reductions) are regressive, and the architecture forces a choice between servicing creditors and funding development. IMF surcharges continue to raise borrowing costs for its most indebted members, with developing countries expected to pay an estimated $5.2 billion in surcharges between 2025 and 2030.

**Both sides accept:** servicing burdens are at multi-decade highs, borrowing costs for these states run several times those of the US or Germany, and the diversion from health, education and investment is real and growing. As IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva told G20 finance ministers, "high refinancing needs and rising debt-service costs are constraining many developing economies… limiting their capacity to finance critical spending on infrastructure, health, and education, which undermines growth and, in turn, debt sustainability."

### Stress test (the benign case)

There is a coherent case that this stays a slow burn rather than a 1980s-style cascade: exposures are fragmented across many small economies and many creditors (now including China and private bondholders), so there is no single domino. Multilateral backstops, the G20 Common Framework, and case-by-case restructurings can keep most countries limping along — as Bolivia's October programme demonstrates. The Senegal arrangement's final approval remains conditional on sufficient corrective action on debt management, fiscal reforms, and firm financing assurances from creditors, illustrating that the system moves slowly but can move. But "no cascade" is not "no crisis" — the human and developmental cost compounds quietly, and a synchronised dollar surge or rise in real yields could still tip several countries at once.

### Historical parallel

The 1980s Latin American debt crisis — the "lost decade" — is the template: foreign-currency debt plus a strong dollar and high global rates produced a decade of austerity, lost growth and political upheaval, ultimately resolved only by the Brady Plan's debt write-downs years later. Senegal's hidden-debt revelation echoes a recurring pattern from that era: official statistics that concealed the true scale of borrowing until a new government opened the books. The lesson is that delay is itself a policy with a body count, and resolution tends to come late and via principal reduction, not endless rollovers.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Dozens of poorer countries now spend more on debt interest than on health or education, squeezing billions of people. Since mid-2026, Bolivia secured a multilateral rescue and Senegal disclosed billions in hidden debts and began restructuring talks — both episodes show the system can respond, but slowly and at high social cost. It is a real, ongoing crisis: unlikely to trigger a global financial crash, but a quiet humanitarian disaster and a driver of migration and instability that rich-world voters and markets persistently underestimate.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**This one is mostly awareness and values,** not a portfolio move — but understanding it helps explain global migration pressures and political instability.

**Watch the US dollar and real yields.** A stronger dollar and higher real yields mechanically raise the local-currency cost of foreign-currency debt for dozens of countries at once — a useful signal even for your own emerging-market exposure.

**Know what your EM bond funds hold.** If you invest in emerging-market or frontier-market debt, you carry exposure to these dynamics; read your fund's factsheet to understand which countries feature prominently.

**Avoid adding leverage in any part of your portfolio** at a time when global real borrowing costs are elevated — the same rate environment that squeezes frontier sovereigns can catch over-leveraged households.

**If you care about the issue,** debt-relief and development charities and campaigns are where individuals can most directly engage.
:::

### What to watch

- The US dollar index and US real yields: at 2.63% as of late October 2026, real yields remain at levels that materially raise financing costs for emerging-market borrowers. Any further climb is a warning sign.

- The Senegal IMF Executive Board vote: approval (and the terms of the bondholder negotiation that follows) will be a test of whether the G20 Common Framework can resolve rather than merely defer a mid-sized African case.

- Sovereign bond spreads and the count of new IMF programmes or missed coupons: the indicator eased in October, but the pipeline of countries under stress has not shrunk.

- Commodity and food prices: importers' fiscal space lives and dies here, and the 2026 Middle East energy shock remains a background pressure for fuel-importing developing economies.

### Why it is under-covered (globally)

Outside Europe it reads as domestic French politics. But France is the eurozone's second-largest economy, and roughly 55% of its government bonds are held by foreign investors — the group most sensitive to bad news. When France wobbles it is a euro-area problem, not just a Paris one. Early signs of contagion beyond France to other European sovereigns and credits are already visible. The unrest comes at a particularly fragile moment for France, where public anger over living costs and deteriorating services is colliding with political instability and a fiscal crisis that threatens to turn into a debt crisis as government borrowing costs rise to the highest level in decades.

### The debate

**The worriers argue:** serial government collapses, a structural deficit that keeps slipping its own targets, frozen pension reform, social unrest, and a 10-year yield above 5% mean France cannot credibly stabilise its debt. Each political crisis ratchets borrowing costs higher, and the interest bill is now large enough — projected at €91 billion in 2027 — to crowd out other spending and create its own vicious circle. [Established]

**The relaxed argue:** France still has a long average debt maturity (around nine years) that blunts the immediate pass-through of higher rates; the ECB's Transmission Protection Instrument stands as a credible backstop against speculative attacks; and political incentives to pass the 2027 budget are strong ahead of a presidential election in April 2027. [Contested]

**Both sides accept:** the political system has chronic difficulty delivering consolidation, debt is rising, the market now charges France a standing "political risk premium," and — [Forecast] — the spread will remain elevated and volatile through the April 2027 presidential election regardless of the November budget outcome.

### Stress test (the benign case)

The eurozone has something the 2012 crisis lacked: a credible central-bank backstop. The mere existence of the ECB's TPI deters speculative self-reinforcing attacks, and France's long maturities mean higher rates feed through slowly. Fitch noted that if parliament cannot reach an agreement within 70 days, the government could still adopt the budget by ordinance, reducing the risk of an extended impasse. The real risk is not a sudden default but a slow erosion: a higher permanent interest bill, an electorate increasingly attracted to parties that explicitly reject consolidation, and tail scenarios (a snap election or early end to Macron's term) that could reprice risk faster than any backstop calms it.

### Historical parallel

The 2010–12 eurozone sovereign-debt crisis is the obvious reference, and the October 2026 spread spike brought France's borrowing premium to its widest against Germany since that era — uncomfortably close to the 2011 peaks. But the ECB's post-2012 "whatever it takes" toolkit substantially changes the likely ending. A sharper cautionary tale is the UK's September 2022 gilt crisis: a credible advanced economy saw its bond market seize within days once confidence in fiscal management cracked. France is not there — but at a 10-year yield above 5% and an OAT–Bund spread briefly touching 178 basis points, the distance to that kind of disorderly repricing is shorter than it was six months ago.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Since February 2026, when France's budget drama seemed to be calming down, the situation has become measurably worse. Nationwide riots, a deficit target already missed, borrowing costs at their highest since 2002, and a bond-market spread at its widest since the 2011 eurozone crisis have all arrived at once. France — the eurozone's second-largest economy — is paying more to borrow than at any point in over two decades, and two of three major credit agencies now officially signal things could deteriorate further. The immediate test is a budget vote at the start of November, with the presidential election in April 2027 creating both a political pressure valve and a source of ongoing uncertainty. A full-blown crisis remains unlikely as long as the European Central Bank's backstop holds, but the slow erosion of fiscal credibility has real costs: higher interest bills, less money for public services, and a currency that carries a rising risk premium.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Check your European exposure.** If you hold international bond or balanced funds, it is worth reading your fund statement to understand how much is allocated to eurozone government debt and how that is distributed across countries.

**Diversify geography as a concept.** Concentrating savings in any single country or currency bloc means carrying that country's political risk; spreading across regions is a basic buffer — not a market call.

**Watch the euro if you hold it or earn in it.** Persistent political risk premiums can weigh on the currency over time, affecting the purchasing power of euro-denominated savings or income.

**Maintain a cash buffer in your own currency.** Eurozone stress can transmit quickly to global credit and equity markets; having liquid reserves in your home currency reduces the pressure to sell at the worst moment.

**Take the general lesson.** Political gridlock has a measurable price — higher borrowing costs and slower fiscal repair — which is a useful lens for assessing any government's finances, including your own country's.
:::

### What to watch

- **The OAT–Bund spread** (and OAT vs Italian BTP): the live fever chart of French risk — already at decade-plus highs; a sustained breach above 180 basis points would be a serious warning signal.
- **The 3 November National Assembly vote on the 2027 budget's revenue half**, and whether Lecornu opts for Article 49.3 again and whether any censure motion then gathers the 289 votes needed to topple him.

## 12. Central banks are quietly buying gold and trimming the dollar {#s12_gold_dedollar}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Every saver — because it’s a signal about long-run confidence in the dollar and government debt, and it shapes the value of money you hold.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 4/10 — Moderate** *Structural signal, not a near-term threat*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** The IMF COFER release of 30 September 2026 confirmed the USD share of allocated global FX reserves at 56.70% at end-June 2026 (Q2 2026), down from 57.13% in Q1 2026; gold prices have eased further to around $3,953/oz by early November 2026. No indicator changed band; the threat level remains unchanged.

**Why this level:** A real, multi-decade reserve shift, but slow-moving with no imminent trigger; more a confidence signal than an acute risk. **Raises it:** A new reserve-weaponisation event; a disorderly dollar decline; faster non-dollar trade settlement. **Lowers it:** A stable dollar reserve share; orderly diversification; renewed official demand for Treasuries.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** The world’s central banks have bought more than 4,000 tonnes of gold between 2022 and early 2026 — the largest sustained official accumulation in modern history. They bought 863 tonnes in 2025 (down from ~1,090 in 2024 only because record prices made them more selective, not less committed). Gold hit an all-time high near \$5,600/oz in late January 2026 before easing to around \$3,950 by early November 2026 [Established].

**\[Established\]** Meanwhile the US dollar’s share of global currency reserves has fallen to roughly 56.8% (late 2025) from about 72% in the early 2000s — the lowest since 1994 — a steady, multi-decade drift that has continued even when the dollar was strong. The 2026 World Gold Council survey (a record 76 central banks) found 45% plan to add gold and 74% expect the dollar’s reserve share to fall further.

**\[Single-source\]** Strikingly, Morgan Stanley research notes gold now makes up a larger share of central-bank reserves than US Treasuries for the first time since 1996. **\[Established\]** Much of the buying is under-reported — China discloses only small amounts even as its gold imports run far higher — so official tallies understate the trend.

### Why it is under-covered

Reserve management is the dullest-sounding topic in finance, and “gold price hits record” gets covered as a market story, not a monetary one. The real signal — the people who manage national wealth quietly reducing dollar dependence and removing counterparty risk — is a slow architectural shift that rarely makes front pages until it’s far advanced.

### The debate

**The “de-dollarization” camp argues:** this is a structural turn away from a dollar that has been “weaponised” through sanctions; gold can’t be frozen or inflated by another government, so sovereigns are buying insurance against US financial power and debt.

**The “just diversification” camp (incl. Fed research) argues:** the dollar still dwarfs all rivals at ~57% of reserves, no currency can replace it, and gold buying is prudent hedging — not the end of the dollar system.

**Both sides accept:** the dollar’s reserve share has fallen for 25 years, central-bank gold demand is at historic highs, and no single currency is filling the gap the dollar is vacating.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Nothing here implies a dollar collapse. The dollar remains dominant in trade invoicing, debt issuance, and payments; reserve shifts unfold over decades; and a 57% share is still overwhelming. Gold buying can simply be sensible diversification after a generation of over-concentration. The caution is directional: reserve-currency transitions historically show up in the data long before they register in public consciousness, and a steady bid for gold over Treasuries is the same “who will buy government debt?” question from Section 1, seen from the official-sector side.

### Historical parallel

The sterling-to-dollar handover across the first half of the 20th century is the model for how slowly reserve status changes — measured in decades, not quarters. The 1970s breakdown of the gold-anchored Bretton Woods system is the reminder that monetary regimes do shift, and that gold tends to be where capital hides when faith in paper claims wavers.

::: box
**In plain terms**

The institutions that manage countries’ savings — central banks — have been buying gold at the fastest pace in modern history and slowly holding fewer dollars. They started in earnest after the US froze Russia’s reserves in 2022, which taught everyone that dollars held abroad can be switched off. This doesn’t mean the dollar is finished — it’s still by far the most-used currency — but it’s a quiet vote of reduced confidence in holding everything in dollars and government IOUs. For an ordinary saver, it’s a signal worth noting about the long-run value of cash and debt, and why a little diversification can make sense.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Understand gold’s role before buying any.** It’s a diversifier and a hedge against currency and crisis risk — not a guaranteed money-maker. It pays no income and can fall sharply (it already dropped from ~$5,600 to ~$3,950 between January and November 2026).

**Keep any allocation modest.** Many advisers think of single-digit percentages of a portfolio; concentrating heavily in gold is its own risk.

**Beware markups and scams.** Physical-gold dealers can charge large premiums, and “rare coin” pitches are a classic trap. Low-cost, well-known gold funds avoid storage and authenticity headaches; if you want physical, compare premiums and use reputable dealers.

**Take the broader hint:** diversifying across currencies and real assets is a reasonable response to long-run concerns about debt and inflation — gold is one option among several, not a silver bullet.
:::

### What to watch

- The IMF COFER data on the dollar’s reserve share, released quarterly.

- World Gold Council quarterly demand reports and the annual central-bank survey.

- Whether large holders (especially China) report more of their actual buying.

- Any moves toward non-dollar trade settlement among major economies.

## 13. The retirement math nobody wants to say out loud {#s14_pensions}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Everyone who hopes to retire — especially younger workers and anyone relying on a state pension or Social Security.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 4/10 — Moderate** *Large but distant*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** No material change since the last edition: the June 2026 SSA Trustees Report projection (OASI depletion Q4 2032, ~22% automatic cut) stands unchanged, reform bills remain in committee, and no pension-fund leverage event has occurred. The Milliman PPFI public-pension funded ratio edged down to 87.4% as of 30 September 2026 after September market losses, a modest deterioration that keeps the story in the same band.

**Why this level:** A near-certain, very large shortfall — but dated to the 2030s–2050s with no near-term trigger; the risk is political delay, not imminent collapse. **Raises it:** Abrupt benefit-cut or trust-fund headlines; a pension-fund leverage event (cf. UK 2022); reform-driven instability. **Lowers it:** Early, gradual reforms; productivity and immigration gains; rising funded ratios.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Populations are ageing faster than retirement systems were designed for. Across the OECD there are about 33 people aged 65+ for every 100 working-age adults today, heading toward 52 by 2060. In the US, the number of workers per retiree fell from 5.1 in 1960 to a projected ~2.1 by 2030. Pay-as-you-go systems — where today’s workers fund today’s retirees — strain as that ratio falls.

**\[Forecast\]** The World Economic Forum estimates the six largest pension systems face a combined retirement-savings gap of about \$224tn by 2050, with the US alone accounting for ~\$137tn (over 60%), and reckons the global gap widens by roughly \$28bn every day. **\[Established\]** Global pension assets across 22 major markets hit a record \$68.3tn at end-2025 — large, but the six biggest systems hold only around 25 cents for every dollar they are projected to owe by 2050.

**\[Established\]** Concrete near-term example: the US Social Security retirement trust fund is projected, in the Trustees' June 2026 report, to be depleted in late 2032, after which incoming taxes would cover only about 78% of scheduled benefits — an automatic cut of roughly a fifth absent reform. US state and local pensions carry unfunded obligations estimated above \$1.2tn.

### Why it is under-covered

It’s the ultimate slow-motion story — a problem dated to the 2030s–2050s that no politician is rewarded for fixing today, because every fix (later retirement, higher contributions, lower benefits) is unpopular. So it’s perpetually deferred, and coverage spikes only around flashpoints.

### The debate

**The crisis camp argues:** the arithmetic is unforgiving — the WEF likens it to “the financial equivalent of climate change” — and decades of postponement mean the eventual adjustments (benefit cuts, tax rises, later retirement) will be abrupt and painful.

**The manageable camp argues:** gradual, well-designed reforms — raising retirement ages in line with longevity, higher contributions, immigration, productivity growth, and broader workforce participation — can close much of the gap if started early; many systems are adjusting.

**Both sides accept:** dependency ratios are deteriorating, pre-funding is insufficient, and reform is politically toxic — which is why it keeps being delayed.

### Stress test (the benign case)

This is a known, forecastable, gradual problem — which is exactly why it’s solvable. Countries that started early (raising retirement ages, building funded pillars) have stabilised their systems; productivity gains (including from AI) could lift the wage base; and immigration can soften dependency ratios. The risk is political, not arithmetic: reforms that destabilise governments get watered down, and gaps compound while leaders postpone — so the burden lands hardest on those who didn’t save privately to fill it.

### Historical parallel

Two warnings. Politically, France’s 2023 attempt to raise the retirement age nearly toppled the government, and the UK’s 2022 pension-fund (LDI) blow-up showed even “well-funded” systems can fracture — both in countries considered relatively healthy. Structurally, Japan is the live preview: the most-aged major economy, already managing decades of demographic decline, shows both the strain and the slow adaptations the rest of the world is heading into.

::: box
**In plain terms**

People are living longer and having fewer children, so there are fewer workers supporting each retiree. The pension and Social Security systems built for a younger world are short of money — by trillions, over the coming decades. It’s not a crisis tomorrow, but the longer governments wait, the harsher the eventual fix: later retirement ages, higher taxes, or smaller benefits. In the US specifically, Social Security’s main fund is on track to run out in late 2032, which would automatically trim benefits by roughly a fifth unless Congress acts. The practical takeaway: assume you may get somewhat less, somewhat later, than promised — and plan for it.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Read your annual statement.** In the US, your Social Security statement (at ssa.gov) shows your projected benefit; check it. Workers elsewhere should locate their state-pension forecast. Knowing the number is step one.

**Capture every bit of free money.** If an employer matches retirement contributions, contributing at least enough to get the full match is the closest thing to free money in finance.

**Start early and automate.** Because of compounding, money saved in your 20s–30s does far more work than the same amount later. Automatic monthly contributions beat willpower.

**Plan for “less, later.”** It’s prudent to assume retirement ages may rise and public benefits may be trimmed; building your own savings buffer protects you if they are.

**Know what kind of pension you have.** A “defined-benefit” pension promises a set payout (employer bears the risk); a “defined-contribution” plan (like a 401(k)) does not — you do. That changes how much you need to save yourself.

**Diversify your retirement income** across sources (state benefit, workplace plan, personal savings) so no single shortfall sinks your plans.
:::

### What to watch

- Official trust-fund depletion dates (e.g. the annual US Social Security Trustees report).

- Retirement-age and contribution reforms — and the political turbulence around them.

- Pension-fund funding ratios (public and corporate) — the Milliman PPFI public-pension funded ratio stood at 87.4% as of September 2026 — and any leverage strategies (the UK 2022 lesson).

- Birth-rate and immigration trends — the long-run drivers of the dependency ratio.

## 14. China is exporting its deflation — the global transmission nobody frames clearly {#s5_china_deflation}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone who buys goods, works in manufacturing, or invests — China's price spillover reshapes inflation, jobs, and trade politics worldwide, and is now running in both directions at once.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 7/10 — High** *Entrenched and bifurcating*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#E8710A"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** The most important change since 29 June 2026 is a sharp swing in China's factory-gate prices: the producer price index (PPI), which was +3.9% year-on-year at the last edition, accelerated to +5.0% in September 2026 — its fastest pace in over four years and well above the 4.4% consensus forecast — driven by surging oil and energy costs linked to Middle East tensions. This changes the story's character: the risk is no longer purely deflationary export pressure but a *bifurcated* one in which China may begin exporting cost-push inflation in energy-intensive goods even as domestic consumer demand and the underlying overcapacity problem persist. At the same time, the trade surplus marched to a record-breaking pace, hitting $133.5 billion in September alone and $938 billion in the first nine months of 2026, on track to exceed 2025's record $1.2 trillion. Anti-dumping and tariff actions by the US and EU continued to escalate throughout the period. The threat level remains at 7/10 (High) because neither the demand-side root cause nor the protectionist feedback loop has eased, even as the direction of price transmission has become harder to read.

**Why this level:** A massive, growing trade surplus, structural domestic demand weakness, entrenched overcapacity, and an accelerating tariff/anti-dumping cycle that has not materially dented export volumes — all compounded by a new, energy-driven cost-push element radiating from Chinese factory gates. **Raises it:** A continued surge in PPI that passes through into global goods inflation, triggering a harder protectionist response; a managed yuan depreciation; any local-government or banking stress event that deepens the demand deficiency. **Lowers it:** Genuine, sustained consumption stimulus sufficient to rebalance China's domestic demand; a negotiated trade de-escalation with the EU and US; a moderation in energy prices that reverses the PPI spike.

---

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** China's structural demand deficiency — rooted in a multi-year property crash now in its fifth year — continues to drive the world's second-largest economy towards its lowest growth target in decades. China's official GDP growth target for 2026 is 4.5–5%, and despite headline growth near that level, deflation persists and consumers are reluctant to spend. In 2025, consumer prices were flat overall, while core inflation rose only 0.7%, as consumer confidence remained soft.

**\[Established\]** A critical development since the last edition is a sharp and unexpected acceleration in producer prices. China's PPI rose 5% year-on-year in September 2026, sharply up from August's 3.8% and beating the 4.5% rise expected, climbing at its fastest pace in over four years as higher fuel prices pushed up costs for businesses. Higher prices were also seen in the coal sector, due to increased demand for winter heating, temporary tight supply in major coal-producing areas, and expectations of rising international coal prices. This acceleration — driven by energy costs rather than by a genuine recovery in domestic demand — changes the nature of the spillover risk: China may now export cost-push inflation in energy-intensive goods to the rest of the world even while its consumer economy remains weak and its underlying overcapacity continues to depress the prices of manufactured goods.

**\[Contested\]** Some analysts see the higher PPI as pointing to an economy gradually moving away from deflationary pressures, even if the recovery remains uneven. Others caution that the move is entirely energy-driven and tells us little about the structural demand deficiency. Headline CPI is forecast to edge up to only around 0.8% in 2026, remaining well below the government's 2% target and reflecting weak domestic demand rather than genuine price recovery.

**\[Established\]** With domestic demand still insufficient to absorb capacity, China's export machine has continued to run at record pace. China's trade surplus widened to $133.53 billion in September 2026 alone — the second-largest monthly surplus on record — and reached $938 billion in the first nine months of the year, putting the annual figure on track to surpass last year's record of $1.2 trillion. The surplus is increasingly driven by higher-value exports: semiconductor shipments rose 108% year-on-year and automobile exports rose 50%.

**\[Established\]** The tariff and anti-dumping response by trading partners continued to escalate throughout the period. The US imposed roughly 260% combined anti-dumping and countervailing duties on Chinese van-type trailers (effective 28 October 2026), and a Section 301 forced-labour tariff of 12.5% on all Chinese goods imposed in late July 2026 remains in force. The EU imposed definitive anti-dumping duties on multiple Chinese product categories between September and October 2026 and opened new investigations into furfuryl alcohol and polyvinylidene fluoride. Despite this pressure, shipments to the US surged nearly 40% year-on-year in September, and the US trade deficit with China rose to $34 billion — evidence that tariffs alone have not materially dented China's export performance.

**\[Single-source\]** Generous tax breaks for exporters, which cost China's finance ministry an estimated $318 billion in 2025, have helped fuel the export boom.

**\[Established\]** The domestic side of the equation shows little sign of turning. Although Beijing has deployed large fiscal resources to stimulate domestic demand, the housing market — the starting point for any meaningful consumption recovery — remains trapped in a downturn, and policymakers' capacity to further support the property sector is diminishing. Officials allocated 250 billion yuan in this year's fiscal budget to subsidise a consumer trade-in programme, alongside a 100 billion yuan fund to support private investment and consumer spending — but economists note the pace of measures will remain incremental, with policymakers viewing the need for aggressive consumption stimulus as low so long as exports continue to power growth.

---

### Why it is under-covered (as a global story)

China's deflation is covered — as a China story. What is under-framed is the transmission: a $20-plus trillion economy quietly exporting both disinflation and, now, energy-driven cost-push pressure into global tradable goods reshapes prices, margins, and politics in dozens of other countries, feeding tariff fights, squeezing emerging-market manufacturers, and complicating every other central bank's job. The bifurcation of the price signal — cheap manufactured goods and expensive energy-intensive inputs arriving simultaneously from the same origin — makes the story harder to narrate and easier to ignore.

---

### The debate

**The catastrophist view:** a debt-deflation spiral that ends in a balance-sheet crisis or a sharp devaluation.

**The stagnation view:** an "L-shaped" grind — low growth, persistent underlying deflation, no collapse — Japanification with Chinese characteristics, now overlaid with an energy-price shock.

**Beijing's view:** risks are contained; "new quality productive forces" (EVs, solar, robotics, AI, semiconductors) will replace property as the engine, and the export surge validates the strategy.

**The new complication:** China's producer inflation has swung higher as escalating hostilities in the Middle East sent oil prices surging and goods related to AI became more expensive — introducing a cost-push channel that sits alongside, and partly masks, the structural deflationary one.

**All sides accept:** the underlying consumer-demand response has been modest relative to the problem, the export surplus is straining trading-partner relations, and the tariff cycle is intensifying even as it fails to slow shipment volumes.

---

### Stress test (the benign case)

China has confounded collapse calls for over a decade. It retains enormous policy room (rates, reserve requirements, special bonds), a closed capital account that limits flight, and genuine global leadership in EVs, solar, semiconductors, and automation that could yet rebalance growth toward higher-value activity. Some analysts argue that recent US-China trade talks and headway in negotiations between Beijing and Brussels should foster a comparatively steady trading climate through the end of 2026. The yuan has actually *strengthened* modestly since the last edition (USD/CNY moved from 6.77 to around 6.65), which reduces, for now, the risk of a managed depreciation amplifying the deflationary export. The flaw in the benign case: every quarter of deferred demand-side reform deepens the debt-deflation loop, widens the external imbalance that provokes a protectionist backlash, and — as the September PPI print shows — leaves China more exposed than ever to external energy shocks it cannot fully absorb domestically.

---

### Historical parallel

Japan after 1990 is the canonical comparison: a property and balance-sheet bust followed by decades of deflationary stagnation despite heavy investment. The cautionary twist is scale — Japan was a creditor exporting capital; China is a far larger share of global manufacturing, so its domestic dynamics radiate outward with greater force. A newer parallel is relevant: the 1970s oil shocks, when a commodity-price surge from outside an economy passed through factory gates and into consumer prices with a lag, causing central banks to misread the inflation signal. The September 2026 PPI spike risks the same misread in reverse: policymakers in importing countries may see energy-driven cost-push from Chinese goods and tighten, while the underlying deflationary excess capacity continues to build.

---

::: box
**In plain terms**

China's economy remains stuck in a slump where underlying demand is weak and factories keep producing more than Chinese consumers can buy. To cope, it exports huge volumes of goods — a trend now running at record pace. But a new twist has appeared: soaring energy prices in 2026 have pushed Chinese factory-gate prices sharply higher, which could mean some imported goods from China get more expensive even as others stay cheap. For shoppers, workers, and policymakers around the world, the China price problem has become harder to read: it is no longer simply "cheap goods flooding in" but a mix of cheap manufactured goods and dearer energy-intensive ones, all wrapped in an escalating trade-barrier dispute that hasn't yet slowed the flood.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**As a shopper, the picture is mixed.** Cheap imports may continue in some categories, but tariff escalation and energy-driven cost-push can raise prices in others — don't assume the low-price trend is permanent or uniform.

**If your job or industry competes with Chinese exports** (manufacturing, steel, solar, EVs, semiconductors), watch for continued pricing pressure even as input costs rise on the energy side.

**For your household finances, maintain a cash buffer.** Trade-war escalation and sudden tariff changes can unsettle markets and supply chains with little warning.

**As a concept, diversification matters more than ever.** Broad exposure concentrated in sectors most sensitive to China's export surge — or to a sudden reversal in energy prices — carries outsized risk.

**Read your own statements and utility bills.** The transmission of China's price shifts arrives through goods prices, energy costs, and eventually mortgage and savings rates — understanding which direction pressure is coming from helps you plan.
:::

- **China's PPI monthly releases** (next: October 2026 data, scheduled for release 9 November 2026): whether the September energy-driven spike persists, moderates, or broadens into non-energy manufactured goods — this determines whether the spillover is cost-push inflation or a temporary blip masking ongoing deflation.

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 7/10 — High** *Coercive tool on a fragile truce*

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** China controls roughly 90% of global rare-earth permanent-magnet production and about 91% of refining and separation — the processing chokepoint, not just the mines. Through 2025 it built a licensing regime into a coercive instrument: April 2025 controls on seven heavy rare earths and magnets; an October 2025 expansion adding five more elements and, via Announcement 61, an extraterritorial reach over foreign goods containing Chinese-origin material; then a November 2025 pause (Announcements 70/72) that suspended the October measures but preserved the April controls and the licensing machinery; that pause, nominally expiring 10 November 2026, has been verbally extended to 10 January 2027 following the Trump–Xi Washington summit, though as of 2 November 2026 no matching numbered MOFCOM legal text had been published [Single-source for legal gap; extension itself [Established]].

**\[Established\]** The key insight from trade data: availability is not access. China’s overall magnet exports rose even as shipments to the US fell sharply, because supply is selectively allocated through discretionary licensing. US yttrium imports ran 17 tons across April–December 2025 versus 333 tons in the prior period; aerospace makers have rationed material. Ford halted Explorer production for a week in May 2025; European magnet prices reached up to six times Chinese levels; by late October 2026, ex-China dysprosium oxide was assessed at roughly 15–19 times the Chinese domestic price [Single-source].

### Why it is under-covered

Headlines treat each tariff truce as “resolved.” The structural reality — that the November détente is a temporary, revocable pause sitting atop intact controls, and that the real bottleneck is processing know-how that takes years to replicate — gets lost between summit news cycles.

### The debate

**The hawk view:** decouple fast; subsidise an ex-China chain across mining, separation, metallisation and magnet-making at any cost, because the leverage is intolerable.

**The realist view:** diversification is possible but slow, capital-heavy and chemistry-bound; switching costs run into billions per firm and decoupling could cut first-year operating profits 15–50% in exposed firms.

**Beijing’s framing:** conservation and national security.

**All sides accept:** the chokepoint is real, processing (not ore) is the binding constraint, and meaningful displacement is measured in years.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Diversification is genuinely underway: a US–Australia critical-minerals framework, a layered US–Japan partnership (including deep-sea rare-earth mud research), nearly \$4bn in US EXIM letters of intent, and new magnet capacity coming online in summer 2026. Substitutes (iron-nitride magnets, magnet-free motor architectures) are advancing. The benign case says prices stay elevated but supply chains adapt through triage and recycling. Its weakness: every credible analysis still puts true processing independence years away, and a single licensing freeze can outpace the build-out.

### Historical parallel

China’s 2010 rare-earth embargo against Japan is the precedent: it spurred a wave of patenting and some ex-China capacity, but did not close the gap quickly — dependence persisted for years. The deeper analogue is the 1973–74 oil shock, which is exactly why the IEA has launched a Critical Minerals Security Programme modelled on the emergency-stockpile cooperation that followed the oil embargo. The lesson: chokepoint shocks accelerate diversification but reward the holder for a long interim.

::: box
**In plain terms**

China controls about 90% of the processing for the ‘rare earth’ magnets used in EVs, electronics, wind turbines, and weapons. It has turned export licenses into a bargaining chip — supply can be quietly throttled even when the minerals technically exist. A November 2025 truce paused the harshest measures but left the controls in place, and building alternatives outside China takes years, so this leverage isn’t going away soon.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Mostly awareness:** expect occasional shortages, price bumps, or delays in products that rely on these magnets (some cars, electronics) when controls tighten.

**If you’re in an affected industry** (autos, electronics, defense, clean energy), supply-chain diversification and inventory planning matter.

**Treat single-stock ‘rare earth’ bets with the usual caution;** it’s a long-running theme, not a guaranteed winner.

**No need to stockpile anything personally** — the impact shows up as prices and availability, not a household emergency.
:::

### What to watch

- Chinese export-license approval times and volumes by element and destination — the real-time gauge of access vs availability.

- Whether the January 2027 extension is formalised in a numbered MOFCOM announcement — as of 2 November 2026 only a MOFCOM official statement and a US Treasury Secretary verbal announcement underpin it — and whether a further renewal is agreed before 10 January 2027.

- Heavy rare-earth prices (dysprosium, terbium, yttrium) and ex-China premiums.

## 16. The lithography chokepoint: one machine the whole digital world needs {#s19_lithography}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Everyone who uses any modern technology, and anyone invested in AI or chips — every advanced chip on Earth depends on one company’s machines, made with one supplier’s optics, in one country.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 6/10 — Elevated** *Extreme single point of failure*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```


**Since the last edition:** No material change since the last edition: cross-strait activity remains at the same elevated level (daily PLA sorties and vessel transits, no new large-scale exercise), export controls continue to tighten on both sides, and advanced fab concentration in Taiwan is unchanged. The threat level remains Elevated.

**Why this level:** The most concentrated chokepoint in the entire economy — one company, one optics supplier, key fabs in Taiwan — though there is no imminent disruption. **Raises it:** A Taiwan crisis or an incident at ASML (Veldhoven) or Zeiss; a sharp escalation of export-control retaliation. **Lowers it:** Geographic diversification of advanced fabs (US, Japan, Europe) maturing; stable cross-strait and export-control conditions.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Every advanced chip in the world — every AI accelerator, every flagship phone processor — is made using extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines built by exactly one company: ASML, in the Netherlands. ASML has a 100% monopoly on EUV and ~94% of lithography overall; Nikon and Canon exited advanced lithography years ago. ASML can build only ~50–60 of these machines a year — a physical ceiling on how much leading-edge chip capacity the world can add. Each next-generation High-NA machine costs around \$400 million.

**\[Established\]** The concentration runs deeper than one company. ASML itself depends on a single optics supplier — Germany’s Zeiss — for the flawless mirrors EUV requires; the future pace of the technology is effectively gated by what Zeiss can build. The leading-edge chips themselves are made overwhelmingly by TSMC in Taiwan (which also depends on Taiwan’s water, per Story 20). So the entire digital economy funnels through one machine-maker, one optics-maker, and one island.

**\[Established\]** This chokepoint is also a weapon. EUV machines have never been sold to China, and US-led export controls keep tightening; China’s share of ASML sales fell from ~33% in 2025 toward ~19–20% in 2026. China is reportedly a decade or more behind on domestic EUV. **\[Contested\]** In April 2026 TSMC said it would stick with current-generation EUV through at least 2029 rather than buy High-NA immediately — a reminder that even the indispensable supplier has only one buyer big enough to absorb its bleeding edge, a mutual dependence that cuts both ways.

### Why it is under-covered

ASML is the most strategically important company almost no one outside the industry can name. The chokepoint is abstract (a lithography machine in Veldhoven) and has been stable for years, so it hides in plain sight — until a Taiwan scenario or a single-site incident makes its fragility suddenly concrete.

### The debate

**The fragility camp argues:** this is the ultimate single point of failure — one firm, one optics supplier, one island — and a disruption (war over Taiwan, an accident, escalating controls) would halt advanced-chip progress for the whole world.

**The resilience camp argues:** the very irreplaceability creates caution on all sides (no one benefits from breaking it), diversification is underway (new fabs in the US, Japan, and Europe), and older chips can carry much of the economy for a long time.

**Both sides accept:** the concentration is extreme and real, processing/manufacturing know-how takes decades to replicate, and meaningful diversification is years away.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Mutual dependence is stabilizing: ASML needs TSMC as much as TSMC needs ASML, China is deterred from a move on Taiwan partly because it would destroy the thing it wants, and a multi-year push is spreading advanced fabs across the US, Japan, and Europe. Older-node chips — which run most of the physical economy — are far less concentrated. The vulnerability: none of that helps in the tail event. A single bad day in the Taiwan Strait, in Veldhoven, or at Zeiss has no quick substitute, and “years away” diversification is no comfort on the day it matters.

### Historical parallel

The 2020–22 chip shortage — when a shortfall of even cheap, mature chips idled auto plants worldwide — was a preview of how a narrow bottleneck cascades through the real economy. The deeper analogue is the oil chokepoints of the 1970s: a single concentrated input, once disrupted, reorders the global economy and triggers a scramble for alternatives that takes a decade to bear fruit.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Every cutting-edge computer chip — in AI systems, phones, everything — is made using a machine that only one company on Earth knows how to build: ASML, in the Netherlands. That company in turn depends on a single supplier (Zeiss in Germany) for its precision mirrors, and the chips themselves are mostly made by one company in Taiwan. So the entire digital world runs through one machine-maker, one optics-maker, and one island. It’s been stable for years, and everyone has reasons not to break it — but it’s the most concentrated single point of failure in the global economy, and a war over Taiwan or a serious accident would have no quick fix.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**This is awareness, not an action item** — but it explains why ‘Taiwan risk’ is treated as a global-economy risk, not a regional one.

**Mind concentration in your investments.** AI and chip exposure ultimately funnels through a very small number of irreplaceable companies and one geography; broad diversification (across regions and sectors) is the realistic hedge for an ordinary investor.

**Expect tech-hardware price and availability swings** whenever export controls or cross-strait tensions escalate; big-ticket electronics purchases can be worth timing around obvious flashpoints.

**Don’t try to trade the geopolitics.** Tail-risk timing is a fool’s errand; a cash buffer and diversification protect you better than predicting a Taiwan crisis.
:::

### What to watch

- Cross-strait tensions and any military escalation around Taiwan — the dominant tail risk.

- Export-control rounds and Chinese retaliation (and China’s share of ASML sales).

- Progress and yields at new advanced fabs outside Taiwan (US, Japan, Europe) — real diversification vs announcements.

- ASML order backlog and High-NA adoption timelines — the health of the chokepoint itself.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Globalization runs through a handful of narrow passages, and the most important of them — the Strait of Hormuz — has been effectively closed to commercial shipping since late February 2026, when the US–Israel conflict with Iran began. Before the war, roughly 25% of the world's seaborne oil trade and 20% of the world's liquefied natural gas passed through the Strait of Hormuz. The International Maritime Organization reported in April that about 20,000 mariners and 2,000 ships were stranded in the Persian Gulf due to the closure. A brief truce collapsed in July; the US reimposed its naval blockade on 14 July 2026, and it remains in place.

**\[Established\]** The physical closure is now compounded by a record surge in violence. October was the most violent month for commercial shipping since the war began, with at least 30 vessels struck through 27 October, roughly double September's 15 confirmed incidents. Attacks on ships in October set a monthly record, and separately Oman rescued sailors from a burning ship near the strait. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed two tankers struck naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and UKMTO reported a vessel struck by an unknown projectile on 1 November. [Single-source on mine claims; no maritime authority has confirmed them.]

**\[Established\]** The market consequences are severe. Crude oil shipping costs have climbed above \$20 a barrel from about \$3 a year earlier; on major Middle East routes they now exceed \$35 a barrel. It now costs \$77 million to hire a very large crude carrier (VLCC) to move US oil to Asia, compared with \$9.2 million in 2025, adding roughly \$38.50 per barrel to delivery costs. Gulf oil exporters have adapted partly: export volumes recovering to near pre-war levels by September through bypass routes such as Fujairah and pipeline diversions, but only 60% of those barrels crossed Hormuz, compared with 83% before the war. ABC News has noted growing concern that a prolonged closure could affect prices for helium, fertiliser, and other goods beyond crude oil.

**\[Established\]** The same chokepoints are also **digital** chokepoints — and that threat has sharply escalated. Around 95% of intercontinental data travels through undersea cables, and they cluster through the same narrow waters. Iranian officials have described plans to strike cables and obstruct repairs; on 21 October, Yadollah Javani, deputy head of political affairs for the IRGC, warned that seven major internet cables beneath the Strait of Hormuz could be targeted if Iranian infrastructure came under attack. Windward identifies six operational cable systems crossing the waterway, including 2Africa, AAE-1, FALCON, Gulf Bridge International, Tata TGN-Gulf, and OMRAN/EPEG. One Iranian official stated that Tehran would also seek to prevent repairs to damaged cables to prolong disruptions. An AI-generated video circulated by Iranian-linked accounts on 27–28 October depicting attacks on Gulf undersea cables followed by disruptions to banking and communications networks. No cable has been cut as of 2 November 2026. **\[Contested\]** — TeleGeography notes that less than 1% of global bandwidth actually transits Hormuz, limiting the global impact of a Hormuz-specific cable cut; multiple cuts could nevertheless disrupt financial transactions, cloud services, e-commerce, and internet access across affected Gulf markets.

**\[Established\]** Other chokepoints remain under pressure. Red Sea tanker volumes fell roughly one-third, from 1.2 million to 806,000 tonnes, as ships rerouted around the Cape. The Panama Canal and Malacca Strait remain sensitive (see previous edition); Panama's port dispute with Chinese-linked operators has not been fully resolved. The deeper structural shift stands: disruption no longer needs a formal blockade — drones, mines, ship-to-ship attacks, and insurance spikes can achieve the same effect far more cheaply, as the October record demonstrates.

---

### Why it is under-covered

The tanker-attack angle receives daily headlines; the cable angle still almost never does, even though a coordinated cable disruption could hit Gulf digital economies as hard as the current shipping paralysis is hitting energy. These are the literal arteries of trade and data, owned mostly by private firms, governed by weak international frameworks, and invisible until they are cut. The shipping-cost pass-through to consumer goods — helium, fertiliser, electronics — is also rarely explained, even though it is already feeding inflation.

---

### The debate

**The fragility camp argues:** the world rebuilt itself around just-in-time logistics, open sea lanes, and a few cable corridors; that assumption is now broken. Deniable, cheap attacks — drones, mines, propaganda videos — can choke trade, energy, and data at multiple points simultaneously, and eight months of Hormuz closure has already proved the system cannot absorb a sustained shutdown without severe cost.

**The resilience camp argues:** the adaptation has been remarkable. Gulf exporters rebuilt flows to near pre-war volumes through pipelines, offshore transfers, and bypass ports; ships rerouted around the Cape after the Red Sea was contested; oil markets, though expensive, have not collapsed. The resilience camp notes that Iran's cable threat is partly deterrence signalling rather than operational intent, and that less than 1% of global bandwidth actually transits Hormuz.

**Both sides accept:** trade and data are concentrated through a handful of chokepoints, redundancy and protection are thin, the cost of disrupting them has fallen dramatically, and the bill for running the world's energy system around a shut Hormuz is now being paid, visibly, through shipping costs and oil prices.

---

### Stress test (the benign case)

The system is more adaptable than it looks. Gulf oil exports rebounded to roughly 16.5 million bpd in September — near pre-war levels — through pipelines, bypass ports, and extensive ship-to-ship transfers, even as Hormuz vessel traffic stayed well below normal; the system is more expensive and increasingly stretched, but it has not collapsed. A durable ceasefire and US blockade-lift could allow a rapid resumption of direct Hormuz transits; prediction markets as of early November put the probability of Hormuz returning to normal traffic by the end of 2026 at around 12%. **\[Forecast\]** The vulnerability remains simultaneity and speed: rerouting works only if one chokepoint fails at a time, and cable cuts give no warning. A compounding event — Hormuz attacks plus Red Sea closure plus a cable cut — would overwhelm the buffers the system has painfully assembled.

---

### Historical parallel

The 2021 grounding of a single container ship in the Suez Canal blocked roughly 12% of global trade for six days and snarled supply chains for months. The 1956 and 1967 Suez closures reordered global shipping for years. The current Hormuz closure — now eight months old and counting — is in a different category: the longest peacetime-equivalent chokepoint disruption since the Suez closures of the Cold War era, and the first to be deliberately compounded by mine-laying, drone attacks on ships, and explicit threats to the digital cables running through the same water. The lesson each of these episodes taught — and which the world keeps relearning — is that chokepoints concentrate efficiency in good times and catastrophe in bad ones.

---

::: box
**In plain terms**

Almost everything you buy travels by sea, and almost all internet data travels through cables on the ocean floor — and both funnel through a few narrow passages, the most critical of which is the Strait of Hormuz. That passage has been functionally closed for eight months: fewer than 10 ships a day are transiting where 85 once did, tanker attacks set an all-time monthly record in October, and oil is now more than \$27 a barrel higher than it was four months ago. Exporters have partly worked around the blockade through pipelines and long ocean detours, which is why the lights are still on — but at a steeply higher cost that is flowing through to fuel, fertiliser, and goods prices. Now Iran has explicitly threatened the undersea cables running through the same strait, adding a second dimension to the crisis: a cut could disrupt banking, cloud services, and communications across the Gulf region within hours, and repairs take weeks. This is the most active crisis in this report, with no clear diplomatic exit visible.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Expect fuel and goods prices to stay elevated and volatile.** With Hormuz still shut and tanker attacks at a record, energy-shock inflation will continue to show up at the pump and in shipping-dependent goods; this is not a brief spike.

**Keep a basic resilience kit.** Communications outages — including internet disruption from cable problems — are a real risk in the Gulf region and beyond; the standard advice applies: some cash on hand, a little extra of essential medications and supplies, and offline copies of critical documents.

**Don't over-react in markets to each headline.** Shipping and energy shocks are already substantially priced in; panic-trading geopolitical news rarely helps. A cash buffer and avoiding leverage (see Story 1) are more useful than trying to time the crisis.

**For businesses,** this is the case for mapping your supply chain's chokepoint exposure, building sensible buffer inventory, and checking whether your IT suppliers rely on Gulf-region cable or data-centre infrastructure — just-in-time logistics and cloud dependencies both carry hidden chokepoint risk.
:::

### What to watch

- **Strait of Hormuz transit counts and vessel attacks** (IMF PortWatch daily data; UKMTO incident reports) — any further collapse toward zero, or a genuine diplomatic reopening, is the single most important signal.
- **Cable-threat escalation** — any confirmed cut to the six cable systems threading Hormuz; Iranian military signalling ahead of any resumption of US–Israeli strikes.
- **Oil shipping costs and the Brent–WTI spread** — the cost pass-through to consumers and the spread between the Gulf-exposed benchmark and the US inland benchmark are the clearest market gauges of chokepoint stress.
- **Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb transit volumes and Panama Canal governance** — a second major chokepoint seizing at the same time as Hormuz remains the central stress-test scenario.

# Section 5 — Physical & Resource Limits {#section-5}

*Physical & Resource Limits (Stories 18, 19, 20, 21)*

The final section grounds the whole brief in physical reality: the digital, electrified economy is colliding with hard limits in power, metal, water, and food. AI's power problem and the grid bottleneck, copper's structural surplus and mine disruptions, the underpricing of water, and the concentrated fragility of food and fertiliser are stories where no amount of financial engineering changes the binding constraint — you cannot print electrons, copper, water, or calories.

Since June, the section has grown both louder and more internally contradictory. On one side, the AI power crisis (Story 18) has become a political battle: New York's data-centre moratorium has been followed by restricting orders across dozens of states, PJM's capacity auction left a 6.8 GW shortfall for the 2028/29 delivery year — the first such failure — and FERC largely struck down PJM's proposed curtailment-first fix, leaving the liability question unresolved [Established]. On the food side (Story 21), disruption to oil supply from the region, coupled with other production pressures, has sent energy-related costs rocketing, which flows directly into fertiliser inputs: ships have been struck in the Strait of Hormuz nearly every day in October, and the FAO Food Price Index averaged 136.0 points in September 2026, its highest since November 2022 [Established]. On the other side, Taiwan's reservoirs have recovered to roughly 59.2% of capacity (Story 20), easing one acute chip-hub risk, and the copper market remains in surplus through 2027 per the ICSG's October forecast (Story 19). The physical limits here are arithmetic, not sentiment, which is precisely why they are priced slowly — and why the Hormuz closure is the clearest demonstration in years of what happens when a physical constraint suddenly becomes binding.

## 18. AI’s power problem: the grid is the new bottleneck {#s15_ai_power}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Every household with an electricity bill — and every investor exposed to AI, because power, not chips, is now the limit on the whole buildout.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 7/10 — High** *The binding constraint on the AI boom*

```{=html}
<div class="meter"><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#2E7D32"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#8BC34A"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#F5A623"></span><span style="background:#E8710A"></span><span></span><span></span><span></span></div>
```

**Since the last edition:** The political and regulatory battle over who pays for AI's electricity appetite has intensified sharply. New York Governor Hochul signed the nation's first statewide data-centre moratorium on 14 July 2026, and at least eleven further governors have since issued restricting executive orders or directives, bringing the total number of moratoria in force across 46 states to around 575. On the supply side, PJM Interconnection's July 2026 capacity auction left the grid 6.8 GW short of its reliability requirement for the 2028/29 delivery year — the first such failure — and PJM's proposed fix, a curtailment-first framework that would have cut data centres off first during emergencies, was largely struck down by FERC on 9 October 2026, leaving the question of who pays wide open. A Department of Energy emergency order issued on 12–16 October 2026 expired without triggering any data-centre curtailment, easing the immediate pressure reading slightly, though structural tightness remains acute.

**Why this level:** Power has become the single biggest constraint on the AI buildout; household electricity bills are rising and turning political; grid and transformer lead times are measured in years; and a regulatory vacuum has opened after FERC rejected PJM's main cost-allocation mechanism. **Raises it:** A 6.8 GW capacity shortfall confirmed for 2028/29; FERC's rejection of PJM's curtailment plan leaving ordinary ratepayers without protection; widening state moratoria signalling that political resistance is hardening. **Lowers it:** The October 2026 DOE emergency order expired without any customer impacts; FERC accepted a large-load registry giving regulators better visibility; and some efficiency and demand-moderation trends remain visible.

---

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** The constraint on artificial intelligence has shifted from chips to electricity. US data-centre electricity demand has jumped from around 23 GW in 2023 toward 42 GW in 2026, and the wait to connect a new power source to the grid has crept up further: the median project built in 2025 took 61 months from interconnection request to commercial operations, and grid interconnection queues remain the primary bottleneck for new supply. Building a data centre takes about two years; new transmission can take a decade. The mismatch is the story.

**\[Established\]** The capacity crunch has moved from forecast to fact. PJM's latest capacity auction left the grid operator 6.8 GW below its Reliability Requirement for the 2028/29 delivery year, the first time the system has failed to clear. New load growth in PJM is 94% data centres, and PJM expects roughly 70 GW of new large load could come to the system by 2038. Even if new power plants are built over the next 15 years, the PJM grid could fall short of demand six times per decade on average.

**\[Established\]** Households are paying for it. Residential electricity prices rose around 42% over five years — faster than overall inflation — and far more in data-centre hotspots. Capacity prices in PJM surged from $28.92 per megawatt-day in 2024/25 to $329.17 per megawatt-day in 2026/27. The PJM Independent Market Monitor directly attributed $6.3 billion in costs from the July 2026 capacity auction to new data-centre load. It has become a political issue: on 14 July 2026, New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed an executive order establishing the nation's first statewide moratorium on new large-scale data centres. The pause, which will last up to a year, is the first statewide ban on data centres, which have drawn increasing concern from lawmakers and citizens based on their impact on electricity prices and the energy grid. By October 2026, moratoria had spread to around 575 locations across 46 states.

**\[Established\]** The regulatory framework meant to manage the crisis has fractured. PJM proposed an Interim Resource Adequacy Service (IRAS) — a plan to let data centres connect to the grid faster in exchange for being cut off first during emergencies. On 9 October 2026, FERC rejected the core of that plan, keeping only a 50 MW large-load definition and a new registry, and rejecting the IRAS curtailment service and bring-your-own-capacity rules. The decision gives large technology companies the green light to keep shifting the enormous costs of data-centre electricity demand onto everyday consumers, and struck down PJM's plan to make data centres bring their own new capacity or face curtailment during grid emergencies. FERC's rejection signals that plans to rein in data-centre costs must now be driven exclusively by states.

**\[Contested\]** How binding is the limit in the near term? A DOE emergency order issued 12–16 October 2026 expired without triggering data-centre curtailment, and PJM stated no customer impacts were needed. About 55 GW of proposed solar, battery, wind, and natural gas projects have cleared PJM's interconnection queue, but still face permitting, financing, and grid-upgrade hurdles. Analysts continue to warn of "phantom" interconnection requests inflating the demand forecasts.

### Why it is under-covered

"AI is booming" is the headline; "the capacity auction failed for the first time" and "the regulator struck down the only plan to make data centres pay" are not. The bottleneck lives in substations, interconnection queues, FERC dockets, and utility rate cases — unglamorous, technical, and slow — even though the outcome may decide whether the AI capex boom described in Story 6 actually earns a return, and whether the regional-bank balance sheets examined in Story 7 survive the commercial real estate consequences of a power-constrained buildout.

### The debate

**The shortage camp argues:** demand is real and exploding, the grid physically cannot keep up, the first capacity-market failure in PJM's history confirms it, and FERC's rejection of the curtailment framework leaves ordinary consumers without protection. Power scarcity will throttle AI, raise everyone's bills, and hand a structural edge to China, which already generates more than twice as much electricity as the United States.

**The sceptic camp argues:** forecasts are inflated by speculative and double-counted interconnection requests; the DOE October emergency order expired quietly without any curtailment; efficiency gains and a capex reappraisal are already visible; and much of the "crisis" is utilities and generators talking their book into higher regulated returns.

**Both sides accept:** grid and transformer lead times are long, residential bills are rising, the first PJM capacity-market failure is a matter of record, and data centres are now a first-order driver of US electricity demand for the first time in decades.

### Stress test (the benign case)

If hyperscalers fund their own power (on-site gas, nuclear, behind-the-meter) and states fill the regulatory vacuum left by FERC's rejection, households can be largely shielded and the buildout proceeds without a grid crisis. PJM's backstop capacity auction, launched in autumn 2026, could yet close the 6.8 GW gap if new generation clears and permitting moves. The weakness in that case: state-by-state regulation is fragmented and slow; the New York moratorium shows how quickly political resistance can harden into permitting blocks; and a single hot summer with data centres competing against air-conditioning load is the kind of event that turns a slow problem into a sudden one.

### Historical parallel

The closest analogue is the late-1990s–early-2000s telecom and dot-com infrastructure rush, when the physical buildout lagged or overshot the hype. More apt may be California's 2000–01 electricity crisis, which showed how quickly a supply-demand mismatch, plus market-design flaws, can produce price spikes and blackouts. The PJM story now adds a new dimension: a regulator (FERC) that has struck down a market fix on jurisdictional grounds, leaving the problem unresolved and costs socialised onto ordinary ratepayers — very close to the California experience where deregulation left consumers exposed while the structural fix arrived years late.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Building artificial intelligence takes enormous amounts of electricity, and the power grid simply cannot be expanded fast enough — new power lines and the giant transformers they need take years, while a data centre goes up in about two. That gap is no longer theoretical: the largest grid in the United States formally failed to procure enough capacity for 2028/29, with data centres accounting for almost all of the new demand. Meanwhile, the regulator threw out the main plan to make data centres carry their own load during emergencies, leaving the bill — estimated at $6.3 billion in a single capacity auction — to fall on ordinary electricity customers. New York has become the first state to ban new large data centres outright; hundreds of local moratoria have followed. There is a genuine debate about whether the demand figures are inflated, but the capacity shortfall is now on the record.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Expect higher electricity bills** and budget for them, especially if you live near data-centre growth; check whether your utility has a rate case pending, as that is where the cost-sharing decisions are made.

**Look into time-of-use rates and efficiency.** Where utilities offer time-of-use pricing, shifting heavy use (laundry, EV charging) off peak can save money; basic efficiency (LEDs, insulation, a smart thermostat) is the cheapest hedge against bill volatility.

**If you invest in AI themes,** remember power is the bottleneck — the picks-and-shovels may be utilities, grid-equipment makers, and power producers as much as chipmakers; diversify rather than concentrating in a single part of the chain.

**Engage locally if a data centre is proposed near you.** Whether ratepayers or the developer pay for grid upgrades is decided in public utility-commission proceedings and state legislature hearings — those are open to comment, and FERC's October ruling means states are now the primary battleground.
:::

### What to watch

- Whether PJM refiles a revised curtailment framework at FERC, and whether FERC accepts it — the single most consequential regulatory question for ratepayer protection.
- PJM's backstop capacity auction results: whether the 6.8 GW shortfall for 2028/29 is closed, and at what price to consumers.
- State moratorium legislation: the New York legislative bill (Responsible Data Center Development Act) remains undelivered to the Governor; similar bills are advancing in other states, with Texas's moratorium pending a state audit.
- Grid interconnection queue times and high-voltage transformer lead times — the physical speed limit that no regulatory fix can instantly overcome.

**Since the last edition:** The ICSG's October 2026 biannual forecast confirmed the market remains in surplus through at least 2027; mine disruptions at Grasberg and Kamoa-Kakula persisted, and the copper price edged up to roughly $6.54/lb (COMEX) by 2 November 2026 — no change to the threat band.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Copper is the physical backbone of electrification — grids, motors, EVs, and data centers are all copper-hungry — and the supply side is struggling to keep up. A January 2026 S&P Global study, *Copper in the Age of AI*, projects demand rising to 42 million metric tons by 2040 (a ~50% increase) while production peaks around 2030 at ~33 million tons, leaving a ~10-million-ton gap — about 25% below demand — which it calls a “systemic risk for global industries.” Copper veteran Daniel Yergin frames the quandary neatly: copper is the great enabler of electrification, yet the pace of electrification is itself the challenge for copper.

**\[Established\]** Prices already reflect the tension: copper set repeated records in late 2025 after a ~35% gain for the year, and in January 2026 topped \$13,000/ton on the London Metal Exchange for the first time. The supply problem is structural — falling ore grades, rising capital costs, permitting delays, water shortages in Chile, and a thin pipeline of new mines — compounded by disruptions at giant mines that have extended into 2026: Grasberg Block Cave in Indonesia averaged only ~141,000 tonnes per day throughput in Q3 2026 (roughly 67% of normal capacity), with a full restart pushed to end-2027 or early 2028; Kamoa-Kakula in the DRC produced ~76,400 tonnes of saleable copper in Q3 2026, trending toward the lower end of its annual guidance, against a design capacity of ~380,000–400,000 tonnes per year. [Established]

**\[Contested\]** The timing is genuinely debated. The ICSG's October 2026 forecast [Forecast] sees a surplus of ~104,000 metric tons in 2026, widening to ~368,000 mt in 2027, as refined output growth outpaces still-modest demand growth of 1.5%. Goldman Sachs continues to place the real inflection around 2029–2030. So the structural case is widely accepted; the near-term shortage is not yet here.

### Why it is under-covered

Copper is boring until it isn’t. It lacks the glamour of AI or the drama of oil, and the deficit is dated to the late 2020s, so it stays a back-page commodity story — even though it is the literal wiring of every other story in this set (the grid, the data center, the EV).

### The debate

**The structural-deficit camp argues:** you cannot electrify the world without roughly doubling copper supply, and the mining industry is structurally incapable of delivering it fast enough — so scarcity and high prices are coming.

**The near-term-balanced camp argues:** right now there is no shortage — inventories are ample and Chinese demand is weak — and high prices plus recycling and substitution (aluminium in some uses) will close more of the gap than the doomers expect.

**Both sides accept:** ore grades are falling, new mines take 10–20 years to permit and build, and long-run demand from grids, AI, and defense is rising fast.

### Stress test (the benign case)

High prices are the cure for high prices: they spur exploration, recycling (which could double), substitution, and efficiency, and a prolonged Chinese slowdown (see Story 14) would soften demand. If supply responds and demand disappoints, the “deficit” arrives later and milder than feared. The vulnerability: the response time is measured in a decade-plus, ore grades keep falling regardless of price, and a cluster of mine disruptions can turn a forecast surplus into a real shortage in a single year.

### Historical parallel

The 2000s commodity supercycle is the template — Chinese industrialization drove copper from under \$2,000 to over \$10,000 a ton before supply caught up. The recurring pattern: structural demand shifts arrive faster than mines can be built, prices overshoot, capital floods in, and supply eventually responds — but the lag is long, and the interim is volatile and expensive.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Electric everything — power grids, electric cars, data centers — needs a lot of copper, and the world is on track to need far more than mines can dig up. New mines take 10 to 20 years to open, ore is getting lower quality, and big mines keep hitting problems. Experts call the looming gap a “systemic risk.” The catch: the real shortage is mostly expected later this decade (around 2029–2030); right now there’s actually plenty of copper and prices, while high, reflect the future more than the present. It’s a slow-moving squeeze, not an emergency — but it’s the wiring under almost every other story in this report.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Mostly awareness:** expect electrification (EVs, grid upgrades, appliances) to stay expensive partly because the raw metal is getting pricier over time.

**If you invest in the energy transition or AI,** copper and other ‘picks-and-shovels’ materials are an under-appreciated way to play it — but commodities are volatile, so size positions modestly and expect a bumpy ride, not a straight line.

**Don’t chase the copper trade on a headline.** The structural story is real but the near-term market is well-supplied; buying after a price spike is how people get hurt in commodities.

**Recycle your electronics and metals.** Recycled (‘secondary’) copper is a genuine part of the solution, and e-waste recycling keeps valuable metal in the system.

## 20. Water: the economy’s most underpriced input {#s17_water}

**WHO THIS HITS:** *Everyone who eats, and anyone near agriculture, chip plants, or data centres — water is the hidden input in food, chips, and the cloud, and it's priced as if it were free.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL: 5/10 — Elevated** *Slow and underpriced, but regionally acute*

**Since the last edition:** Three developments have moved the story since 29 June 2026. First, the India–Pakistan Indus Waters Treaty dispute has sharpened: the Permanent Court of Arbitration ruled on 31 August 2026 that the 1960 pact remains fully binding, but India has rejected the award, described the tribunal as illegally constituted, declined to appear, and maintained that the treaty remains in abeyance. Pakistan's leaders have responded in increasingly stark terms, with senior officials publicly declaring any interference with Pakistan's water share an act of war. Second, the Colorado River system has deteriorated sharply: Lake Mead stood at 1,038.85 ft as of 31 October 2026 — a new all-time record low, 1.65 ft below the previous record set in 2022. Third, the drought picture in Taiwan has substantially improved: as of 22 October 2026, Taiwan's tracked reservoirs held roughly 59.2% of combined capacity and are within their normal operating range, a major recovery from the near-historic lows seen in southern Taiwan earlier in the year. These forces broadly offset each other — worsening geopolitical water risk and record US reservoir stress on one side, easing chip-hub drought risk on the other — leaving the overall level unchanged.

**Why this level:** A slow, structurally underpriced constraint that is already regionally acute (chips, farms, data centres) but lacks a single near-term systemic trigger. **Raises it:** Lake Mead now at an all-time record low, threatening hydropower and water supply for millions in the US Southwest; the India–Pakistan Indus dispute escalating toward open crisis language between two nuclear-armed states; continued underpricing of water globally. **Lowers it:** Taiwan's reservoirs have recovered to normal operating range, removing the acute chip-hub drought risk flagged in the previous edition; efficiency, reuse, and — above all — sane water pricing retain large headroom to ease supply constraints if implemented.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Roughly half the world's population already faces water scarcity, and the Global Commission on the Economics of Water warns that, on current trends, water stress could cut global GDP by around 8% by 2050 (and far more in lower-income countries), with over half of food production at risk. Agriculture uses about 70% of the world's freshwater, so water and food are the same story. The Commission's core economic point is blunt: water is systematically *underpriced*, which encourages waste and pushes water-hungry industries into the wrong places.

**\[Established\]** The collision with the tech boom is direct. An analysis by the TNFD found roughly 40% of existing chip fabs sit in basins projected to face high or extreme water stress by 2030. Taiwan — where TSMC makes roughly 90% of the world's most advanced chips — suffered its worst drought since 1964 in 2020–21, and earlier in 2026 southern Taiwan's largest reservoir, Tsengwen, fell to just 9.6% capacity, only three percentage points above its 2021 record low. By late October 2026, however, Taiwan's reservoirs had recovered to roughly 59% of combined capacity and were within their normal operating range, demonstrating how quickly the situation can swing — in either direction. Data centres add thirst on top: community opposition over water has delayed or blocked tens of billions of dollars of projects globally.

**\[Established\]** The Colorado River system has passed a grim milestone. Record-low snowpack following an exceptionally dry winter, poor runoff across the upper Colorado River Basin, and extreme heat — compounded by long-term drought and sustained water demand — have driven Lake Mead to its lowest level on record. As of 31 October 2026, the reservoir stood at 1,038.85 ft, a new all-time record low, 1.65 ft below the previous record. Lake Powell, the second-largest reservoir in the nation, has also reached its lowest water level in history and is in danger of dropping to "dead pool," at which point its Glen Canyon Dam would lose the ability to produce hydropower entirely. A threshold five feet below Lake Mead's early-October level would trigger immediate cuts to hydropower output and water allocations affecting millions in the Southwest; if that mark were breached, 70% of Hoover Dam's power generation would shut down.

**\[Established/Contested\]** The Indus Waters Treaty dispute between India and Pakistan has moved from legal tension to crisis language. India and Pakistan are locked in a dispute over the Indus Waters Treaty after New Delhi suspended the pact, raising concerns about regional stability. The PCA in The Hague ruled in Pakistan's favour on 31 August 2026, saying the historic pact remains fully binding and that India had no valid grounds for ending or suspending it. The Indian government categorically rejected the ruling. Pakistan says India has also stopped sharing routine river-flow data and blocked site inspections since 2023, both of which the treaty requires. Pakistani leaders have responded with explicit security framing: the Senate Chairman declared at Pakistan Water Week on 2 November 2026 that any attempt to block Pakistan's water share would be treated as an act of war — language that senior ministers had already used in October. The award of 31 August 2026 does not end the Indus Waters dispute, nor does it resolve the political tensions between Pakistan and India.

### Why it is under-covered

Because water is nearly free, it doesn't show up as a market signal until it's a crisis. There is no global "water price" on a screen, so the slow erosion of this input is invisible to markets — right up until a drought stops a chip fab, empties a reservoir, or sparks a cross-border dispute. The Colorado River's new record low has attracted attention in the United States, but the financial implications — for agriculture, hydropower, and the regional economy — have received far less coverage than the engineering spectacle of falling water lines.

### The debate

**The crisis camp argues:** the global water cycle is being pushed out of balance, water is dangerously mispriced, and the result is a slow-building hit to food, industry, and growth — a financial risk markets are ignoring. Lake Mead's new all-time record and the breakdown of a 66-year-old transboundary treaty are symptoms of structural failure, not one-off bad luck.

**The techno-optimist camp argues:** efficiency, recycling and reuse, desalination, precision irrigation, and (eventually) better pricing can stretch supply enormously. Taiwan's rapid reservoir recovery from near-crisis to normal levels in a single monsoon season illustrates how quickly precipitation can reset local conditions. The largest firms can already secure their own water — so this is a manageable engineering and policy problem, not a catastrophe.

**Both sides accept:** water is underpriced, agriculture dominates use, and water-intensive industries are increasingly sited in already-stressed basins.

### Stress test (the benign case)

Water problems are mostly local and solvable: efficiency and reuse have huge headroom, chipmakers already recycle most process water, and pricing reform (where politically possible) would reallocate water quickly. Taiwan's reservoirs recovering from near-historic lows to 59% of capacity within a single season is a reminder that precipitation can move quickly. The vulnerability is that water is intensely political and local: you cannot ship it far or cheaply, drought is getting more frequent, and a single dry year in the wrong basin — the Colorado River, the Indus, a chip-hub river — can disrupt hydropower, food supply, or geopolitics with little warning.

### Historical parallel

History is full of water-driven economic and political upheaval, from the US Dust Bowl of the 1930s to Cape Town's 2018 "Day Zero" near-miss and Chennai running dry in 2019. The Colorado River's own history offers a cautionary tale: the 1922 Colorado River Compact allocated far more water than the river actually carries in most years — a legal fiction that has compounded for a century and now leaves two of America's largest reservoirs at all-time lows. The recurring lesson: societies treat water as infinite until a sharp shortage forces brutal rationing and reveals how much of the economy was silently resting on it.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Water is the invisible ingredient in food, computer chips, and data centres — and because it's priced as though it were free, we waste it and build thirsty industries in dry places. Farming uses about 70% of fresh water, so water shortages are really food shortages. Since the last edition, two developments stand out. America's largest reservoir, Lake Mead, has hit an all-time record low — threatening hydropower for millions in the US Southwest. :::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**This is mostly awareness** — understanding that food prices, chip supply, hydropower costs, and some regional economies hinge on water helps you read the news (a drought story or a water-treaty breakdown can be an economic story).

**If you live in a water-stressed region,** water-efficient appliances, fixing leaks, and drought-tolerant landscaping cut bills and exposure — and water rates are likely to rise over time as scarcity gets priced in.

**Factor water into big location decisions.** If you're buying property or starting a business, a region's long-term water situation — including its dependence on rivers like the Colorado — is a real and under-considered risk.

**Hold adequate cash buffers.** Disruptions to food or energy supply from water stress (think hydropower cuts at Hoover Dam) can feed through to consumer prices; a liquid financial cushion gives you time to adjust.

**Diversify broadly across geographies and sectors** — concentrated exposure to agriculture, semiconductors, or utilities in water-stressed regions carries a risk that rarely appears on a balance sheet.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** The world's food supply rests on a handful of fertilizer producers and trade routes, and several remain constrained at once. China imposed a near-total phosphate fertilizer export ban from March 2026 through August 2026 to protect domestic supply. Effective from mid-March 2026, China implemented a comprehensive "zero-export" policy covering all mainstream phosphate products including MAP and DAP, scheduled through 31 August 2026. The formal August deadline passed, but the real-trade recovery has been negligible: Argus Media's senior China consultant noted that tight sulfur supplies mean Chinese phosphate fertilizer production is currently focused solely on meeting domestic demand, and Argus expects only a modest rebound to around 2 million tonnes of Chinese phosphate exports next year. Russia continues to enforce quantitative limits on nitrogen and phosphate shipments, and Belarusian potash remains under sanctions; the EU has tariffed Russian and Belarusian fertilizer.

**\[Established\]** The Strait of Hormuz has moved from disrupted to near-closed for commercial shipping. Shipping traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has been largely blocked by Iran since 28 February 2026, when the United States and Israel launched an air war against Iran. IMF PortWatch recorded just 4 transits on 18 October against a pre-crisis baseline of 85 per day, and only 2 transits on 25 October. October was the most violent month for commercial shipping since the war began, with at least 30 vessels struck — roughly double September's 15 confirmed incidents. Some crude oil is moving via US-escorted "dark ship" convoys and ship-to-ship transfers in the Arabian Sea, with US forces having escorted over 3,000 commercial vessels through the strait since the summer, but fertilizer-specific inputs — sulfur, ammonia, urea — lack comparable protection. The Food Policy Institute has warned of long-term increases in food prices due to disruption in fuel and fertilizer markets, as the Strait is central to the global fertilizer trade.

**\[Established\]** The food-price signal is now flashing more urgently. FAO attributes the September rise to transportation disruptions and weather concerns that constrained availability, as well as higher quotations for crop-based commodity groups. The FAO Cereal Price Index averaged 122.8 points in September, up 5.1 per cent from August and 17.2 per cent above its September 2025 value. The FAO Sugar Price Index increased by 6.1 per cent from August, driven by expectations of tighter global sugar supplies in the 2026/27 season. FAO cut its forecast for world cereal trade in 2026/27 by 3.5 million tonnes to 505.8 million tonnes, 3.5 per cent below the previous season, citing constrained Black Sea shipping routes and insufficient alternative transport capacity.

**\[Established\]** The structural concentrations identified in the last edition remain unchanged and are now being tested in real time. Canada still supplies roughly 90% of US potash imports. Phosphate is doubly squeezed: China is diverting supply into lithium-iron-phosphate EV batteries (a direct link to electrification demand, covered in Story 19), while Morocco holds the dominant share of global phosphate-rock reserves. China's absence from phosphate trade is adding to pressure in import-dependent markets, where a sulfur shortage is already idling Brazilian MAP, SSP and TSP capacity. **\[Contested\]** Estimates that global phosphorus production could peak around 2035 are debated, but the concentration of reserves is not.

### Why it is under-covered

Fertilizer is invisible until food gets expensive. It is a chemistry-and-logistics story most people never think about — yet roughly half the world's food production depends on synthetic fertilizer, and a fertilizer shock today is a grocery-bill and hunger shock 6–12 months later, especially in poorer importing countries (a direct link to the developing-world debt story, Story 10). The Hormuz crisis dominates headlines as an energy and geopolitical story; its fertilizer dimension — sulfur and ammonia flows, not just oil tankers — receives far less attention even as the food-price consequences may outlast the military phase.

### The debate

**The fragility camp argues:** production and trade are dangerously concentrated in a few countries and chokepoints; the Hormuz crisis has exposed just how quickly those concentrations can become acute shortages; China's export curbs, though nominally eased, have left near-zero actual volumes; and the 6–12 month lag from fertilizer shortage to harvest shortfall means the worst food-price consequences are still ahead.

**The reassurance camp argues:** fertilizer markets have shown some adaptation — India secured Canadian supply, Brazil is rerouting — and US naval escorts are keeping some commodity flows moving through Hormuz. US officials have said 95 per cent of traffic in the strait has transited safely under escort arrangements, and high prices will eventually pull new supply forward.

**Both sides accept:** supply is concentrated, prices are at a near four-year high, key inputs still run through contested waters, and the lag from fertilizer to food means disruptions now show up in next year's harvests.

### Stress test (the benign case)

The system has shown some adaptability even under the current shock: US naval escorts have kept a reduced volume of oil and some commodity flows moving, and trade has partially rerouted to avoid the Hormuz bottleneck. Argus forecasts a gradual rebound in Chinese phosphate exports once sulfur supply is more secure. New fertilizer capacity in Canada and elsewhere is still arriving. The vulnerability is correlation and timing: if Hormuz violence persists into the Southern Hemisphere planting season while China's export recovery stalls and a major harvest disappoints, the buffers thin fast — and the people hurt first are those who can least afford it.

### Historical parallel

2022 is the fresh memory: Russia's invasion of Ukraine spiked fertilizer and grain prices, drove food inflation worldwide, and contributed to unrest in import-dependent countries. The current situation has a meaningful parallel but is structurally distinct — the Hormuz crisis has simultaneously cut a key energy and fertilizer-input corridor while China has withdrawn from phosphate trade, creating overlapping shocks rather than a single commodity disruption. Further back, the 2007–08 food-price crisis — amplified by export bans cascading across producers — triggered riots across dozens of countries. The pattern is consistent: fertilizer and grain shocks transmit into political instability with a lag, and export bans make a shortage worse by converting a supply problem into a panic.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Most of the world's food depends on fertilizer, and fertilizer comes from just a few countries and travels through a few sea routes — several of which are now simultaneously restricted or under attack. China imposed a near-total phosphate export ban through August 2026; the ban has formally expired, but actual exports remain close to zero because China's own sulfur supply is also constrained by the Gulf crisis. Meanwhile, the Strait of Hormuz — through which large volumes of sulfur, ammonia, and urea pass — has been largely closed to commercial shipping since late February, with October seeing the highest number of vessel strikes since the conflict began. The UN food agency's benchmark price index has risen for three consecutive months to its highest level since November 2022, with cereals up more than 17 per cent year on year. The effects on harvests from reduced fertilizer use will not be fully visible until 2027, but the direction of travel is clear: those feeling it hardest first will be lower-income households and countries that depend on food imports.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Expect food-price volatility** over the next 12–18 months and build a little flexibility into your grocery budget; staples — grains, cooking oils, and foods relying on natural gas or phosphate inputs — are the most exposed.

**A modest food buffer is reasonable; panic-buying is not.** A couple of weeks of shelf-stable staples is sensible household resilience; hoarding makes shortages worse for everyone, as the 2008 crisis demonstrated.

**Reduce food waste.** Roughly a third of food is wasted globally; cutting your own waste is the most direct personal contribution to easing pressure on the system and protecting your own budget.

**If you garden or can buy local,** even a small amount of home or locally-sourced food production adds resilience to global supply shocks that are beyond your control.

**Read your household budget like a balance sheet.** Rising grocery costs hitting at the same time as broader inflation means a cash buffer and reduced discretionary debt give you room to absorb higher staple costs without being forced into difficult trade-offs.

# Pattern analysis: how the five mechanisms interlock {#pattern}

Read together across this edition, the twenty-one stories are no longer five loosely coupled layers — they are showing signs of active, simultaneous co-movement for the first time since this brief began. The mechanism is simpler than it looks: a physical chokepoint (Story 17) generated an oil price shock; that shock produced inflation that could not be solved by monetary policy; the Fed hiked into a supply shock anyway, lifting the rate level across the curve; and that higher rate level is now stressing every interest-rate-sensitive story in the brief simultaneously. That is the new architecture of risk in this edition.

Follow the chain. The Strait of Hormuz closure (Story 17) removed roughly a quarter of global oil flows [Established]. A complete cessation of oil exports from the Persian Gulf corresponds to a disruption of roughly 20% of global oil supplies, which the Dallas Fed's model estimated would raise US headline inflation by 1.7 percentage points annualised in the first quarter of 2026. That is a supply problem that rate hikes cannot fix. The Fed hiked regardless in September, pushing the effective policy rate to 3.75%–4.00% [Established]. That hike, combined with the energy-inflation premium already embedded in long-end yields, pushed the 10-year Treasury above 5% (Story 1) and the MOVE index above 100 [Established]. The MOVE surge widened the basis trade's risk profile; the higher rate level directly increased debt-service costs for floating-rate private credit borrowers (Story 3), which is why that story's default rate more than doubled to 6.3% in a single year [Established]. The same rate level is the ceiling against which \$1.26 trillion in CRE debt must refinance in 2027 (Story 7) [Single-source], and is part of the mechanism widening France's OAT–Bund spread (Story 11), since French debt carries a long duration and a high refinancing need.

Analysts have noted that contagion in European government bonds has switched direction — it is now France driving spread pressure onto the periphery, rather than the periphery driving pressure onto France. For now, the risk of market stress escalating sharply and spreading to the real economy appears limited, with better-capitalised banks and low household debt helping contain contagion. That is the honest qualification: the stresses are real but not yet cascading. The ECB backstop, better-capitalised European banks, and the absence of a full sovereign funding crisis are material buffers. Similarly, the US private credit system, while under growing strain, has not yet experienced a liquidity event that forces mark-to-market losses into the open. The basis trade's SOFR–EFFR widening is mild. The CRE maturity wall is a 2027 problem that is not today's problem.

What has changed is the *combination* rather than any single indicator. Six months ago, high Treasury yields, private credit stress, France's fiscal deterioration, and the Gulf closure were each a separate, containable concern. Today they share a common mechanism — the rate level — and are therefore harder to address one at a time. Every tool that might ease one tends to worsen another: rate cuts that would relieve private credit borrowers (Story 3) and the CRE wall (Story 7) would also risk re-inflating the oil-driven price level (Story 17) and weakening the dollar (Story 12). Fiscal stimulus that might offset the energy-driven demand shock is constrained by the spreads that stimulus would itself widen (Story 11). This is the loss-of-firefighting-capacity problem that the previous edition identified in the abstract — it is now visible in the data.

The physical layer (Section 5) and the geoeconomic layer (Section 4) remain the upstream generators of these stresses: the Hormuz closure did not emerge from a financial model, it emerged from a geopolitical decision. The rare-earth extension (Story 15), the ASML export controls (Story 16), and Tehran's explicitly documented plans to attack undersea cables if struck (Story 17) are similarly not market events — they are state actions that arrive on political timetables and cannot be hedged away by the financial system. The food and fertiliser fragility (Story 21) and the power-grid shortfall (Story 18) are arithmetic constraints that will bind harder as the AI build-out accelerates.

The honest synthesis for this edition: the system has moved from many separate elevated risks to several risks with a common driver. That common driver — higher long rates sustained by a supply shock the central bank cannot cure — is the thread that, if pulled, connects the leverage (Section 1), the credit marks (Section 2), and the sovereign backstop (Section 3) into a single loop. The tracker's overall rise from 6.5 to 7.8 reflects exactly this: not that any single story has become catastrophic, but that more stories are now correlated with each other than they were six months ago, which is precisely the condition under which a single shock can light several floors at once.

### Connections to watch

- **One rate level, five pressure points.** The same higher-for-longer rate environment that widened the Treasury basis trade (Story 1), doubled private credit default rates (Story 3), stalled CRE refinancing (Story 7), pushed France's OAT yield toward 5% (Story 11), and increased developing-world debt-service costs (Story 10) has a single upstream cause — an oil supply shock (Story 17) that monetary policy can dampen but cannot cure. Watching the 10-year Treasury yield and the MOVE index together tells you how tightly coupled these stories currently are.

- **The Gulf closure reaches the balance sheet through the food chain.** The Strait of Hormuz disruption (Story 17) is not only a trade and energy story: higher diesel and shipping costs transmit directly into fertiliser input prices (Story 21), which lifts the FAO Food Price Index and adds to the same headline inflation that prompted the Fed's September hike — which in turn raised floating-rate private credit borrowers' debt-service costs (Story 3). The household that is already behind on car payments (Story 8) is also paying more at the fuel pump and the supermarket checkout.

- **France is now the contagion source, not the contagion destination.** Whereas France previously fell victim to contagion from the eurozone periphery, the direction has now reversed. Early signs of contagion are visible beyond France to other European sovereigns, with Italian government bond yields widening materially and Spain and other peripheral sovereigns also weakening — meaning Story 11 is no longer contained and has become a driver of the broader monetary-order risks tracked in Story 10 and the rate conditions that underpin Story 1.

- **Private credit sits on both sides of the AI bet, and the Fed hike has tightened both sides simultaneously.** Story 3 treats AI as a threat to private-credit borrowers disrupted by AI; Story 6 shows private credit funding AI data centres through asset-backed loans. The September rate hike (prompted by the Gulf shock in Story 17) raised debt-service costs for software-sector borrowers and for data-centre borrowers at the same moment — hitting the same lenders twice through two different loan books.

- **Energy spikes are a clearinghouse event, not merely a volatility event.** The 2022 gas and nickel spikes demonstrated that commodity price surges generate margin calls large enough to threaten clearinghouse members' liquidity. An oil and gas shock from the Gulf (Story 17) running through commodity clearing models is therefore a direct trigger for Story 5, not merely an indirect one through general volatility. The MOVE index's rise above 100 (Story 1) and the VIX's rise to 21.46 (Story 5) are the early-warning readings; the commodity-margin channel is the more acute potential path.

- **China holds three levers simultaneously, and the Gulf shock has complicated all three.** China's bifurcated price picture — deflation in consumer goods, PPI at +5.0% year-on-year driven by energy costs (Story 14) — means that higher Gulf oil prices (Story 17) are now partly *offsetting* the deflationary export pressure that was the original risk. Meanwhile, China's rare-earth extension (Story 15) and the ASML chokepoint (Story 16) remain intact. An escalation in the chip contest can still arrive as a minerals shock; both feed the trade-retaliation cycle; and the energy cost transmission from the Gulf now makes China's own export economics harder to predict.

- **The same physical constraints that cap AI revenue growth also cap the grid that powers AI.** Hyperscaler capex is growing at over 100% year-on-year (Story 6) while PJM's capacity auction produced the grid's first reliability shortfall for the 2028/29 delivery year (Story 18). The circular financing in Story 6 assumes continued AI revenue growth; that growth requires data-centre power the grid cannot yet guarantee. A grid curtailment event would therefore be simultaneously a Story 18 physical risk and a Story 6 financial risk, arriving as a single event on two sections of this brief at once.

# Confidence, caveats and what could make this wrong {#caveats}

A word on confidence. Throughout this brief, claims are tagged \[Established\] (documented by multiple authoritative sources), \[Contested\] (real but debated in size or interpretation), \[Single-source\] (reported but not yet widely corroborated), or \[Forecast\] (a forward projection, not a fact). The threat levels are analytical judgments on a 1–10 scale, not probabilities, and they describe vulnerability — how much damage a position could do if stressed — not a prediction that it will be. Several of the highest-scored stories may sit quietly for years.

This brief is deliberately organized by mechanism rather than by headline, and that lens is itself a claim: that these twenty-one risks are best understood as five layers of one system whose real danger lies in their correlations. That framing could be wrong in either direction — a shock could stay contained in one layer, or a connection we have not drawn could matter more than the ones we have. The material is informational analysis, not investment, legal or financial advice; it points to what to watch and why things relate, and leaves the decisions to you.

# Plain-language glossary {#glossary}

**Basis trade —** a leveraged trade that profits from the small price gap between US Treasury bonds and Treasury futures, financed with very short-term borrowing.

**Repo —** the overnight market where institutions borrow cash against securities; the funding that keeps much of the leverage in this brief alive.

**Haircut —** the safety margin a lender demands on collateral. A zero haircut means no cushion if prices move against the borrower.

**MOVE index —** a gauge of expected US Treasury-market volatility — the bond market's equivalent of the VIX.

**Carry trade —** borrowing in a cheap, low-rate currency (often the yen) to invest in higher-yielding assets elsewhere; profitable until the funding currency moves.

**Private credit —** loans made by non-bank funds directly to companies, outside public markets and with less frequent valuation.

**Stablecoin —** a crypto token pegged to the dollar, typically backed by reserves that increasingly include US Treasuries.

**Clearinghouse (CCP) —** an institution that stands between buyers and sellers to guarantee trades; efficient in normal times, a concentrated single point of failure in a crisis.

**Maturity wall —** a cluster of debt all coming due in a short window, forcing refinancing at whatever rates then prevail.

**Mark-to-market —** valuing an asset at its current market price rather than its original cost; 'extend and pretend' delays this reckoning.

**Credit spread (OAS) —** the extra yield investors demand to hold risky debt over safe government debt; widening spreads signal rising default fear.

**Sovereign spread —** the gap between one government's borrowing cost and a benchmark (e.g., French OATs over German Bunds); a market gauge of fiscal risk.

**De-dollarization —** the gradual shift by central banks and governments away from the US dollar as a reserve and settlement currency, often toward gold.

**Chokepoint —** a narrow node — a strait, a mine, a single supplier — through which a large share of global supply must pass, giving whoever controls it leverage.

**Grid interconnection —** the queue and physical process for connecting new power demand or supply to the electricity grid; a binding bottleneck for AI data centers.
