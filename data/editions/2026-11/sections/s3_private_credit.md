**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone with money in pensions, insurance products, or 'alternative' funds — private credit has quietly spread into retirement and insurance portfolios.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Record defaults, gates now routine*

**Since the last edition:** Three developments moved the needle since 29 June 2026. First, Fitch's US Private Credit Default Rate (PCDR) hit a record 6.3% for the trailing 12 months ended Q3 2026 — the fourth consecutive quarterly increase — up from 2.73% at the time of the last edition and more than double the long-run historical average. Second, redemption gates at large semi-liquid funds became a recurring, not isolated, event: one flagship fund capped withdrawals at 5% of net asset value for a third consecutive quarter in Q3 2026, with investors seeking exit at roughly twice the permitted rate. Third, on 16 September 2026 the Federal Reserve raised interest rates by 25 basis points to a target range of 3.75%–4.00% — the first hike since 2023 — directly increasing debt-service costs for floating-rate private credit borrowers already under pressure.

**Why this level:** Defaults have now broken decisively above historical norms and are confirmed by a record reading; redemption pressure at major funds has become structural rather than episodic; and a fresh rate rise adds cost pressure to an already stressed borrower pool. **Raises it:** The default rate climbing for four straight quarters with stressed maturity extensions and payment-in-kind arrangements masking the true extent of impairment; gates at funds representing combined assets of roughly $135 billion; a Fed that may hike again; and the insurer channel still opaque. **Lowers it:** Fund-level leverage remains low relative to pre-2008 securitisation structures; lock-ups prevent depositor-style bank-run dynamics; the software sector — once the most feared default source — had the lowest quarterly default rate (0.6%) in Q3; and bank capital buffers remain above stress-test minima.

---

### What is happening

**[Established]** Private credit — non-bank direct lending to mid-sized companies — has grown to roughly $1.5–$2.0 trillion in assets and sits largely outside the prudential regulatory perimeter. The Financial Stability Board has warned that the sector's performance has never been tested in an economic downturn or during a period of prolonged market stress. It cautioned that "valuation opacity and reliance on private credit ratings can amplify strains in stress," noting that the lack of public ratings makes it difficult for regulators to develop a comprehensive view of the market.

**[Established]** The default picture has deteriorated sharply since the last edition. The default rate for US private credit hit a record high in the third quarter; for the trailing 12-month period it came in at 6.3% — the fourth straight quarterly increase. Fitch recorded 27 private credit default events from 20 new unique defaulters in Q3 2026, pushing the trailing 12-month total to 91 unique defaulters. Stressed maturity extensions, interest deferrals, and payment-in-kind introductions continued to account for most default events. Notably, the industrials and manufacturing sector and consumer products and services sector had the highest default rates at 9.7% each, while the software sector — long cited as the most exposed to AI disruption — had the lowest quarterly rate at just 0.6%.

**[Established]** The rate environment has tightened further. On 16 September 2026, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates by 25 basis points to a target range of 3.75%–4.00%, the first hike since July 2023. Companies that borrow through direct lending typically carry floating-rate debt, meaning their interest costs rise automatically whenever the Fed moves rates — and the hike pushes up borrowing expenses at a moment when defaults are already climbing to levels not seen before.

**[Established]** Redemption gates at large funds have become a defining feature of this cycle, not a one-off. In Q3 2026, one major fund opened a new tender offer; investors estimated to have requested approximately 10% of shares outstanding were told the fund would fulfil only 5%, with final repurchase values expected in November. That fund hit its redemption cap for three consecutive quarters. Separately, a $31 billion fund capped redemptions at 5% in Q3 2026 after requests reached 16% — its first gate — while another $25 billion fund prorated for a second consecutive quarter after requests of approximately 11.5%.

**[Established]** The most under-watched channel remains the private-equity-to-insurance linkage. Industry estimates indicate insurers now account for 23.4% of private credit capital providers, representing between $0.9 trillion and $1.1 trillion of investment exposure. The US Treasury Department has assembled a team to assess insurer exposure, while the IMF has warned that insurers holding leveraged private credit instruments could face larger-than-expected losses during periods of stress. CAIA data indicates that 6.4% of private credit loans carried deferred-interest, or "bad PIK," structures in early 2026; and more than one-tenth of loans held by private credit funds had been marked down by at least 50%, according to MSCI data.

---

### Why it is under-covered

It is private by design. There are no daily prices, no public ratings on most borrowers, and net asset values are reported with a 60–90 day lag using model-based marks. Opacity is the product feature that makes the risk hard to see — and hard to report. Private credit remained under scrutiny during the third quarter as investors continued to focus on default risk, older loan vintages, software-sector exposure, and BDC liquidity — yet individual fund-level detail still arrives slowly and inconsistently, and there is no single regulatory body with a consolidated view across jurisdictions.

---

### The debate

**The warning case:** default rates are at a record and rising for four consecutive quarters; the instruments doing most of the masking — PIK arrangements, stressed maturity extensions, and interest deferrals — accounted for the majority of Q3 default events, not outright missed payments; gates are now routine at funds holding more than $130 billion in combined assets; a further Fed rate hike is plausible; and insurer balance sheets carry exposures that regulators are only now beginning to quantify.

**The reassurance case (fund managers, some regulators):** fund-level leverage is modest — roughly 1–1.5x, versus 30–40x in pre-2008 securitisation structures; capital is locked up for years rather than runnable in the way bank deposits are; the Federal Reserve's own May 2026 Financial Stability Report called private credit risks "limited and manageable"; and credit fundamentals across several large funds remain largely stable, with some non-accruals falling quarter-over-quarter.

**Both sides accept:** defaults are now at a record, valuation practices are opaque, the sector is untested through a full prolonged downturn, and redemption gates — while functioning as designed — are preventing investors from accessing capital on the timescales they expected.

---

### Stress test (the benign case)

The structural differences from 2008 are real: long lock-ups prevent depositor-style runs, leverage is far lower, and there is no comparable web of CDOs amplifying losses through the system. The most probable adverse path therefore remains not a sudden collapse but a slow shakeout — rising defaults, forced markdowns, restricted redemptions, tighter credit for mid-sized firms — painful for investors and borrowers but, on current evidence, absorbable by the banking system. Banks hold roughly $95 billion in direct credit exposure to private credit funds and BDCs; even under a severe shock scenario, the Fed's 2025 stress tests showed bank capital ratios holding at an 11.8% minimum. The risk to that benign read has, however, become more concrete: persistently high real interest rates are maintaining pressure on highly leveraged borrowers' debt-service capacity and refinancing options, and a further rate increase — projected by twelve of eighteen FOMC members as of September — would add to that pressure before any relief.

---

### Historical parallel

The unavoidable comparison is subprime — opaque, rapidly grown, model-valued credit — but the analogy is contested and imperfect given the leverage and liquidity differences. A closer fit may be the slow-burn savings-and-loan and leveraged-loan workouts of the late 1980s and early 1990s: not a single detonation, but a multi-year grind of markdowns and restructurings that traps capital and tightens lending well after the headlines fade. Issuers with weak or declining earnings, elevated leverage, and approaching maturities have contributed to increased liability-management exercises, restructurings, and defaults — with refinancing risk continuing to increase as maturities approach for loans originated in 2021–22, when base rates were lower and underwriting assumptions were more aggressive.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Big investment firms have built a roughly $1.5–$2 trillion business lending directly to mid-sized companies — outside the banks and outside daily market prices. The headline risk is now visible in the data: defaults have hit a record for four quarters running, and at several large funds investors who asked to withdraw their money are getting back only half of what they requested, quarter after quarter. A fresh interest-rate rise in September 2026 adds cost pressure to borrowers already struggling. The losses are concentrated in manufacturing, healthcare, and consumer businesses — not tech, as many expected. Because a lot of this money sits inside insurance and retirement products, ordinary people are exposed without necessarily realising it, and the slow pace of reporting means they may be the last to find out.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Check your exposure.** Ask whether your retirement or insurance products hold 'private credit,' 'direct lending,' or 'alternatives,' and read the fund factsheet carefully.

**Be sceptical of 'high steady yield, no volatility.'** Smooth returns in these funds often mean the price simply is not being updated — not that there is no risk. A gate means your money may be locked in for longer than you planned.

**Understand the lock-up before you commit.** Many of these funds can restrict redemptions to 5% per quarter; if 10% or more of investors want out, you may wait several quarters to recover your capital.

**Diversify.** Do not let one yield-chasing product — however well it performed in recent years — become a large share of your savings. The current environment tests exactly the assumptions on which those returns were built.

**Hold a cash buffer.** If private credit or other illiquid alternatives are part of your portfolio, ensure you have sufficient liquid savings elsewhere so that a gate does not force you into a bad decision elsewhere.
:::

---

### What to watch

- **The Fitch PCDR in Q4 2026:** whether it breaches 6.3% or begins to plateau is the single clearest quantitative signal of whether the slow shakeout is stabilising or accelerating.
- **Redemption gate data for Q4 2026:** specifically whether the funds that prorated for three consecutive quarters begin to clear the backlog — or whether the queue of unfulfilled requests grows further into year-end.
- **Further Fed rate decisions:** twelve of eighteen FOMC members projected at least one more hike before year-end as of the September meeting; each additional 25 basis points directly raises the floating-rate cost burden on the most stressed borrowers.
- **Insurer and pension disclosures on private credit concentration:** the channel into retirement savings remains the least transparent part of the system, and the US Treasury team assessing this exposure has not yet published findings.
