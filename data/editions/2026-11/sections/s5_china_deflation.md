**WHO THIS HITS:** *Anyone who buys goods, works in manufacturing, or invests — China's price spillover reshapes inflation, jobs, and trade politics worldwide, and is now running in both directions at once.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *Entrenched and bifurcating*

**Since the last edition:** The most important change since 29 June 2026 is a sharp swing in China's factory-gate prices: the producer price index (PPI), which was +3.9% year-on-year at the last edition, accelerated to +5.0% in September 2026 — its fastest pace in over four years and well above the 4.4% consensus forecast — driven by surging oil and energy costs linked to Middle East tensions. This changes the story's character: the risk is no longer purely deflationary export pressure but a *bifurcated* one in which China may begin exporting cost-push inflation in energy-intensive goods even as domestic consumer demand and the underlying overcapacity problem persist. At the same time, the trade surplus marched to a record-breaking pace, hitting $133.5 billion in September alone and $938 billion in the first nine months of 2026, on track to exceed 2025's record $1.2 trillion. Anti-dumping and tariff actions by the US and EU continued to escalate throughout the period. The threat level remains at 7/10 (High) because neither the demand-side root cause nor the protectionist feedback loop has eased, even as the direction of price transmission has become harder to read.

**Why this level:** A massive, growing trade surplus, structural domestic demand weakness, entrenched overcapacity, and an accelerating tariff/anti-dumping cycle that has not materially dented export volumes — all compounded by a new, energy-driven cost-push element radiating from Chinese factory gates. **Raises it:** A continued surge in PPI that passes through into global goods inflation, triggering a harder protectionist response; a managed yuan depreciation; any local-government or banking stress event that deepens the demand deficiency. **Lowers it:** Genuine, sustained consumption stimulus sufficient to rebalance China's domestic demand; a negotiated trade de-escalation with the EU and US; a moderation in energy prices that reverses the PPI spike.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** China's structural demand deficiency — rooted in a multi-year property crash now in its fifth year — continues to drive the world's second-largest economy towards its lowest growth target in decades. China's official GDP growth target for 2026 is 4.5–5%, and despite headline growth near that level, deflation persists and consumers are reluctant to spend. In 2025, consumer prices were flat overall, while core inflation rose only 0.7%, as consumer confidence remained soft.

**\[Established\]** A critical development since the last edition is a sharp and unexpected acceleration in producer prices. China's PPI rose 5% year-on-year in September 2026, sharply up from August's 3.8% and beating the 4.5% rise expected, climbing at its fastest pace in over four years as higher fuel prices pushed up costs for businesses. Higher prices were also seen in the coal sector, due to increased demand for winter heating, temporary tight supply in major coal-producing areas, and expectations of rising international coal prices. This acceleration — driven by energy costs rather than by a genuine recovery in domestic demand — changes the nature of the spillover risk: China may now export cost-push inflation in energy-intensive goods to the rest of the world even while its consumer economy remains weak and its underlying overcapacity continues to depress the prices of manufactured goods.

**\[Contested\]** Some analysts see the higher PPI as pointing to an economy gradually moving away from deflationary pressures, even if the recovery remains uneven. Others caution that the move is entirely energy-driven and tells us little about the structural demand deficiency. Headline CPI is forecast to edge up to only around 0.8% in 2026, remaining well below the government's 2% target and reflecting weak domestic demand rather than genuine price recovery.

**\[Established\]** With domestic demand still insufficient to absorb capacity, China's export machine has continued to run at record pace. China's trade surplus widened to $133.53 billion in September 2026 alone — the second-largest monthly surplus on record — and reached $938 billion in the first nine months of the year, putting the annual figure on track to surpass last year's record of $1.2 trillion. The surplus is increasingly driven by higher-value exports: semiconductor shipments rose 108% year-on-year and automobile exports rose 50%.

**\[Established\]** The tariff and anti-dumping response by trading partners continued to escalate throughout the period. The US imposed roughly 260% combined anti-dumping and countervailing duties on Chinese van-type trailers (effective 28 October 2026), and a Section 301 forced-labour tariff of 12.5% on all Chinese goods imposed in late July 2026 remains in force. The EU imposed definitive anti-dumping duties on multiple Chinese product categories between September and October 2026 and opened new investigations into furfuryl alcohol and polyvinylidene fluoride. Despite this pressure, shipments to the US surged nearly 40% year-on-year in September, and the US trade deficit with China rose to $34 billion — evidence that tariffs alone have not materially dented China's export performance.

**\[Single-source\]** Generous tax breaks for exporters, which cost China's finance ministry an estimated $318 billion in 2025, have helped fuel the export boom.

**\[Established\]** The domestic side of the equation shows little sign of turning. Although Beijing has deployed large fiscal resources to stimulate domestic demand, the housing market — the starting point for any meaningful consumption recovery — remains trapped in a downturn, and policymakers' capacity to further support the property sector is diminishing. Officials allocated 250 billion yuan in this year's fiscal budget to subsidise a consumer trade-in programme, alongside a 100 billion yuan fund to support private investment and consumer spending — but economists note the pace of measures will remain incremental, with policymakers viewing the need for aggressive consumption stimulus as low so long as exports continue to power growth.

### Why it is under-covered (as a global story)

China's deflation is covered — as a China story. What is under-framed is the transmission: a $20-plus trillion economy quietly exporting both disinflation and, now, energy-driven cost-push pressure into global tradable goods reshapes prices, margins, and politics in dozens of other countries, feeding tariff fights, squeezing emerging-market manufacturers, and complicating every other central bank's job. The bifurcation of the price signal — cheap manufactured goods and expensive energy-intensive inputs arriving simultaneously from the same origin — makes the story harder to narrate and easier to ignore.

---

### The debate

**The catastrophist view:** a debt-deflation spiral that ends in a balance-sheet crisis or a sharp devaluation.

**The stagnation view:** an "L-shaped" grind — low growth, persistent underlying deflation, no collapse — Japanification with Chinese characteristics, now overlaid with an energy-price shock.

**Beijing's view:** risks are contained; "new quality productive forces" (EVs, solar, robotics, AI, semiconductors) will replace property as the engine, and the export surge validates the strategy.

**The new complication:** China's producer inflation has swung higher as escalating hostilities in the Middle East sent oil prices surging and goods related to AI became more expensive — introducing a cost-push channel that sits alongside, and partly masks, the structural deflationary one.

**All sides accept:** the underlying consumer-demand response has been modest relative to the problem, the export surplus is straining trading-partner relations, and the tariff cycle is intensifying even as it fails to slow shipment volumes.

---

### Stress test (the benign case)

China has confounded collapse calls for over a decade. It retains enormous policy room (rates, reserve requirements, special bonds), a closed capital account that limits flight, and genuine global leadership in EVs, solar, semiconductors, and automation that could yet rebalance growth toward higher-value activity. Some analysts argue that recent US-China trade talks and headway in negotiations between Beijing and Brussels should foster a comparatively steady trading climate through the end of 2026. The yuan has actually *strengthened* modestly since the last edition (USD/CNY moved from 6.77 to around 6.65), which reduces, for now, the risk of a managed depreciation amplifying the deflationary export. The flaw in the benign case: every quarter of deferred demand-side reform deepens the debt-deflation loop, widens the external imbalance that provokes a protectionist backlash, and — as the September PPI print shows — leaves China more exposed than ever to external energy shocks it cannot fully absorb domestically.

---

### Historical parallel

Japan after 1990 is the canonical comparison: a property and balance-sheet bust followed by decades of deflationary stagnation despite heavy investment. The cautionary twist is scale — Japan was a creditor exporting capital; China is a far larger share of global manufacturing, so its domestic dynamics radiate outward with greater force. A newer parallel is relevant: the 1970s oil shocks, when a commodity-price surge from outside an economy passed through factory gates and into consumer prices with a lag, causing central banks to misread the inflation signal. The September 2026 PPI spike risks the same misread in reverse: policymakers in importing countries may see energy-driven cost-push from Chinese goods and tighten, while the underlying deflationary excess capacity continues to build.

::: box
**In plain terms**

China's economy remains stuck in a slump where underlying demand is weak and factories keep producing more than Chinese consumers can buy. To cope, it exports huge volumes of goods — a trend now running at record pace. But a new twist has appeared: soaring energy prices in 2026 have pushed Chinese factory-gate prices sharply higher, which could mean some imported goods from China get more expensive even as others stay cheap. For shoppers, workers, and policymakers around the world, the China price problem has become harder to read: it is no longer simply "cheap goods flooding in" but a mix of cheap manufactured goods and dearer energy-intensive ones, all wrapped in an escalating trade-barrier dispute that hasn't yet slowed the flood.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**As a shopper, the picture is mixed.** Cheap imports may continue in some categories, but tariff escalation and energy-driven cost-push can raise prices in others — don't assume the low-price trend is permanent or uniform.

**If your job or industry competes with Chinese exports** (manufacturing, steel, solar, EVs, semiconductors), watch for continued pricing pressure even as input costs rise on the energy side.

**For your household finances, maintain a cash buffer.** Trade-war escalation and sudden tariff changes can unsettle markets and supply chains with little warning.

**As a concept, diversification matters more than ever.** Broad exposure concentrated in sectors most sensitive to China's export surge — or to a sudden reversal in energy prices — carries outsized risk.

**Read your own statements and utility bills.** The transmission of China's price shifts arrives through goods prices, energy costs, and eventually mortgage and savings rates — understanding which direction pressure is coming from helps you plan.
:::

### What to watch

- **China's PPI monthly releases** (next: October 2026 data, scheduled for release 9 November 2026): whether the September energy-driven spike persists, moderates, or broadens into non-energy manufactured goods — this determines whether the spillover is cost-push inflation or a temporary blip masking ongoing deflation.

- **The yuan (USD/CNY) exchange rate**: the yuan has strengthened slightly since the last edition; any reversal toward managed depreciation would amplify exported deflation and invite further retaliation.

- **Export volumes versus values, and the pace of tariff/anti-dumping actions** by the EU, US, and emerging markets: whether volume growth finally slows in response to barrier escalation, or whether China continues to divert trade flows through third countries and new markets such as ASEAN.

- **Any genuine consumption stimulus** — direct household transfers, social safety net expansion, or property floor measures sufficient to revive domestic demand — versus more supply-side investment; this remains the signal of whether Beijing is treating the cause or the symptom.
