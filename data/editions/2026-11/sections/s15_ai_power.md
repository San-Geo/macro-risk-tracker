**WHO THIS HITS:** *Every household with an electricity bill — and every investor exposed to AI, because power, not chips, is now the limit on the whole buildout.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *The binding constraint on the AI boom*

**Since the last edition:** The political and regulatory battle over who pays for AI's electricity appetite has intensified sharply. New York Governor Hochul signed the nation's first statewide data-centre moratorium on 14 July 2026, and at least eleven further governors have since issued restricting executive orders or directives, bringing the total number of moratoria in force across 46 states to around 575. On the supply side, PJM Interconnection's July 2026 capacity auction left the grid 6.8 GW short of its reliability requirement for the 2028/29 delivery year — the first such failure — and PJM's proposed fix, a curtailment-first framework that would have cut data centres off first during emergencies, was largely struck down by FERC on 9 October 2026, leaving the question of who pays wide open. A Department of Energy emergency order issued on 12–16 October 2026 expired without triggering any data-centre curtailment, easing the immediate pressure reading slightly, though structural tightness remains acute.

**Why this level:** Power has become the single biggest constraint on the AI buildout; household electricity bills are rising and turning political; grid and transformer lead times are measured in years; and a regulatory vacuum has opened after FERC rejected PJM's main cost-allocation mechanism. **Raises it:** A 6.8 GW capacity shortfall confirmed for 2028/29; FERC's rejection of PJM's curtailment plan leaving ordinary ratepayers without protection; widening state moratoria signalling that political resistance is hardening. **Lowers it:** The October 2026 DOE emergency order expired without any customer impacts; FERC accepted a large-load registry giving regulators better visibility; and some efficiency and demand-moderation trends remain visible.

---

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** The constraint on artificial intelligence has shifted from chips to electricity. US data-centre electricity demand has jumped from around 23 GW in 2023 toward 42 GW in 2026, and the wait to connect a new power source to the grid has crept up further: the median project built in 2025 took 61 months from interconnection request to commercial operations, and grid interconnection queues remain the primary bottleneck for new supply. Building a data centre takes about two years; new transmission can take a decade. The mismatch is the story.

**\[Established\]** The capacity crunch has moved from forecast to fact. PJM's latest capacity auction left the grid operator 6.8 GW below its Reliability Requirement for the 2028/29 delivery year, the first time the system has failed to clear. New load growth in PJM is 94% data centres, and PJM expects roughly 70 GW of new large load could come to the system by 2038. Even if new power plants are built over the next 15 years, the PJM grid could fall short of demand six times per decade on average.

**\[Established\]** Households are paying for it. Residential electricity prices rose around 42% over five years — faster than overall inflation — and far more in data-centre hotspots. Capacity prices in PJM surged from $28.92 per megawatt-day in 2024/25 to $329.17 per megawatt-day in 2026/27. The PJM Independent Market Monitor directly attributed $6.3 billion in costs from the July 2026 capacity auction to new data-centre load. It has become a political issue: on 14 July 2026, New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed an executive order establishing the nation's first statewide moratorium on new large-scale data centres. The pause, which will last up to a year, is the first statewide ban on data centres, which have drawn increasing concern from lawmakers and citizens based on their impact on electricity prices and the energy grid. By October 2026, moratoria had spread to around 575 locations across 46 states.

**\[Established\]** The regulatory framework meant to manage the crisis has fractured. PJM proposed an Interim Resource Adequacy Service (IRAS) — a plan to let data centres connect to the grid faster in exchange for being cut off first during emergencies. On 9 October 2026, FERC rejected the core of that plan, keeping only a 50 MW large-load definition and a new registry, and rejecting the IRAS curtailment service and bring-your-own-capacity rules. The decision gives large technology companies the green light to keep shifting the enormous costs of data-centre electricity demand onto everyday consumers, and struck down PJM's plan to make data centres bring their own new capacity or face curtailment during grid emergencies. FERC's rejection signals that plans to rein in data-centre costs must now be driven exclusively by states.

**\[Contested\]** How binding is the limit in the near term? A DOE emergency order issued 12–16 October 2026 expired without triggering data-centre curtailment, and PJM stated no customer impacts were needed. About 55 GW of proposed solar, battery, wind, and natural gas projects have cleared PJM's interconnection queue, but still face permitting, financing, and grid-upgrade hurdles. Analysts continue to warn of "phantom" interconnection requests inflating the demand forecasts.

### Why it is under-covered

"AI is booming" is the headline; "the capacity auction failed for the first time" and "the regulator struck down the only plan to make data centres pay" are not. The bottleneck lives in substations, interconnection queues, FERC dockets, and utility rate cases — unglamorous, technical, and slow — even though the outcome may decide whether the AI capex boom described in Story 6 actually earns a return, and whether the regional-bank balance sheets examined in Story 7 survive the commercial real estate consequences of a power-constrained buildout.

### The debate

**The shortage camp argues:** demand is real and exploding, the grid physically cannot keep up, the first capacity-market failure in PJM's history confirms it, and FERC's rejection of the curtailment framework leaves ordinary consumers without protection. Power scarcity will throttle AI, raise everyone's bills, and hand a structural edge to China, which already generates more than twice as much electricity as the United States.

**The sceptic camp argues:** forecasts are inflated by speculative and double-counted interconnection requests; the DOE October emergency order expired quietly without any curtailment; efficiency gains and a capex reappraisal are already visible; and much of the "crisis" is utilities and generators talking their book into higher regulated returns.

**Both sides accept:** grid and transformer lead times are long, residential bills are rising, the first PJM capacity-market failure is a matter of record, and data centres are now a first-order driver of US electricity demand for the first time in decades.

### Stress test (the benign case)

If hyperscalers fund their own power (on-site gas, nuclear, behind-the-meter) and states fill the regulatory vacuum left by FERC's rejection, households can be largely shielded and the buildout proceeds without a grid crisis. PJM's backstop capacity auction, launched in autumn 2026, could yet close the 6.8 GW gap if new generation clears and permitting moves. The weakness in that case: state-by-state regulation is fragmented and slow; the New York moratorium shows how quickly political resistance can harden into permitting blocks; and a single hot summer with data centres competing against air-conditioning load is the kind of event that turns a slow problem into a sudden one.

### Historical parallel

The closest analogue is the late-1990s–early-2000s telecom and dot-com infrastructure rush, when the physical buildout lagged or overshot the hype. More apt may be California's 2000–01 electricity crisis, which showed how quickly a supply-demand mismatch, plus market-design flaws, can produce price spikes and blackouts. The PJM story now adds a new dimension: a regulator (FERC) that has struck down a market fix on jurisdictional grounds, leaving the problem unresolved and costs socialised onto ordinary ratepayers — very close to the California experience where deregulation left consumers exposed while the structural fix arrived years late.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Building artificial intelligence takes enormous amounts of electricity, and the power grid simply cannot be expanded fast enough — new power lines and the giant transformers they need take years, while a data centre goes up in about two. That gap is no longer theoretical: the largest grid in the United States formally failed to procure enough capacity for 2028/29, with data centres accounting for almost all of the new demand. Meanwhile, the regulator threw out the main plan to make data centres carry their own load during emergencies, leaving the bill — estimated at $6.3 billion in a single capacity auction — to fall on ordinary electricity customers. New York has become the first state to ban new large data centres outright; hundreds of local moratoria have followed. There is a genuine debate about whether the demand figures are inflated, but the capacity shortfall is now on the record.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Expect higher electricity bills** and budget for them, especially if you live near data-centre growth; check whether your utility has a rate case pending, as that is where the cost-sharing decisions are made.

**Look into time-of-use rates and efficiency.** Where utilities offer time-of-use pricing, shifting heavy use (laundry, EV charging) off peak can save money; basic efficiency (LEDs, insulation, a smart thermostat) is the cheapest hedge against bill volatility.

**If you invest in AI themes,** remember power is the bottleneck — the picks-and-shovels may be utilities, grid-equipment makers, and power producers as much as chipmakers; diversify rather than concentrating in a single part of the chain.

**Engage locally if a data centre is proposed near you.** Whether ratepayers or the developer pay for grid upgrades is decided in public utility-commission proceedings and state legislature hearings — those are open to comment, and FERC's October ruling means states are now the primary battleground.
:::

### What to watch

- Whether PJM refiles a revised curtailment framework at FERC, and whether FERC accepts it — the single most consequential regulatory question for ratepayer protection.
- PJM's backstop capacity auction results: whether the 6.8 GW shortfall for 2028/29 is closed, and at what price to consumers.
- State moratorium legislation: the New York legislative bill (Responsible Data Center Development Act) remains undelivered to the Governor; similar bills are advancing in other states, with Texas's moratorium pending a state audit.
- Grid interconnection queue times and high-voltage transformer lead times — the physical speed limit that no regulatory fix can instantly overcome.
