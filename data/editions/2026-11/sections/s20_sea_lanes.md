**WHO THIS HITS:** *Everyone — through fuel and goods prices, and through the internet itself. About 80% of trade moves by sea and ~95% of data moves under it, much of it through a few narrow, contested passages.*

**CURRENT THREAT LEVEL:** *A live, deepening energy-and-trade emergency*

**Since the last edition:** The brief ceasefire that was notionally reopening the Strait of Hormuz collapsed in early July when Iran struck multiple commercial ships and the United States reimposed its naval blockade on 14 July; on 8 July, the interim truce between the US and Iran broke down after Iran struck multiple commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz. Since then the strait has remained effectively shut: IMF PortWatch recorded just 2 transits on 25 October against a pre-crisis baseline of 85 per day — Day 246 of the closure. On the cable front, what was previously a veiled threat has hardened into an explicit military plan: Tehran has drawn up detailed plans to attack undersea communications cables in the Persian Gulf if the United States and Israel launch renewed strikes, with one official stating that Iran would also seek to prevent repairs to damaged cables to prolong disruptions. WTI crude oil, which stood at \$68.78 at the last edition, is now at \$96.10 [Established], reflecting a protracted supply shock rather than a brief spike.

**Why this level:** The world's single most important oil chokepoint has been functionally closed for eight months, tanker attacks set a monthly record in October, oil shipping costs have surged from roughly \$3 to over \$20 per barrel on average, cables through the same waters are under explicit military threat, and no credible diplomatic off-ramp is in sight. **Raises it:** A resumption of direct US–Israeli strikes on Iran, which Iranian officials say would trigger immediate cable attacks; a second major chokepoint seizing simultaneously; a confirmed cable cut; continued escalation of ship attacks into an outright shipping halt. **Lowers it:** A durable ceasefire reopening Hormuz; a diplomatic settlement lifting the US blockade; faster-than-expected expansion of bypass routes and pipeline capacity; cable redundancy improving faster than threats materialise.

### What is happening

**\[Established\]** Globalization runs through a handful of narrow passages, and the most important of them — the Strait of Hormuz — has been effectively closed to commercial shipping since late February 2026, when the US–Israel conflict with Iran began. Before the war, roughly 25% of the world's seaborne oil trade and 20% of the world's liquefied natural gas passed through the Strait of Hormuz. The International Maritime Organization reported in April that about 20,000 mariners and 2,000 ships were stranded in the Persian Gulf due to the closure. A brief truce collapsed in July; the US reimposed its naval blockade on 14 July 2026, and it remains in place.

**\[Established\]** The physical closure is now compounded by a record surge in violence. October was the most violent month for commercial shipping since the war began, with at least 30 vessels struck through 27 October, roughly double September's 15 confirmed incidents. Attacks on ships in October set a monthly record, and separately Oman rescued sailors from a burning ship near the strait. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed two tankers struck naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and UKMTO reported a vessel struck by an unknown projectile on 1 November. [Single-source on mine claims; no maritime authority has confirmed them.]

**\[Established\]** The market consequences are severe. Crude oil shipping costs have climbed above \$20 a barrel from about \$3 a year earlier; on major Middle East routes they now exceed \$35 a barrel. It now costs \$77 million to hire a very large crude carrier (VLCC) to move US oil to Asia, compared with \$9.2 million in 2025, adding roughly \$38.50 per barrel to delivery costs. Gulf oil exporters have adapted partly: export volumes recovering to near pre-war levels by September through bypass routes such as Fujairah and pipeline diversions, but only 60% of those barrels crossed Hormuz, compared with 83% before the war. ABC News has noted growing concern that a prolonged closure could affect prices for helium, fertiliser, and other goods beyond crude oil.

**\[Established\]** The same chokepoints are also **digital** chokepoints — and that threat has sharply escalated. Around 95% of intercontinental data travels through undersea cables, and they cluster through the same narrow waters. Iranian officials have described plans to strike cables and obstruct repairs; on 21 October, Yadollah Javani, deputy head of political affairs for the IRGC, warned that seven major internet cables beneath the Strait of Hormuz could be targeted if Iranian infrastructure came under attack. Windward identifies six operational cable systems crossing the waterway, including 2Africa, AAE-1, FALCON, Gulf Bridge International, Tata TGN-Gulf, and OMRAN/EPEG. One Iranian official stated that Tehran would also seek to prevent repairs to damaged cables to prolong disruptions. An AI-generated video circulated by Iranian-linked accounts on 27–28 October depicting attacks on Gulf undersea cables followed by disruptions to banking and communications networks. No cable has been cut as of 2 November 2026. **\[Contested\]** — TeleGeography notes that less than 1% of global bandwidth actually transits Hormuz, limiting the global impact of a Hormuz-specific cable cut; multiple cuts could nevertheless disrupt financial transactions, cloud services, e-commerce, and internet access across affected Gulf markets.

**\[Established\]** Other chokepoints remain under pressure. Red Sea tanker volumes fell roughly one-third, from 1.2 million to 806,000 tonnes, as ships rerouted around the Cape. The Panama Canal and Malacca Strait remain sensitive (see previous edition); Panama's port dispute with Chinese-linked operators has not been fully resolved. The deeper structural shift stands: disruption no longer needs a formal blockade — drones, mines, ship-to-ship attacks, and insurance spikes can achieve the same effect far more cheaply, as the October record demonstrates.

### Why it is under-covered

The tanker-attack angle receives daily headlines; the cable angle still almost never does, even though a coordinated cable disruption could hit Gulf digital economies as hard as the current shipping paralysis is hitting energy. These are the literal arteries of trade and data, owned mostly by private firms, governed by weak international frameworks, and invisible until they are cut. The shipping-cost pass-through to consumer goods — helium, fertiliser, electronics — is also rarely explained, even though it is already feeding inflation.

---

### The debate

**The fragility camp argues:** the world rebuilt itself around just-in-time logistics, open sea lanes, and a few cable corridors; that assumption is now broken. Deniable, cheap attacks — drones, mines, propaganda videos — can choke trade, energy, and data at multiple points simultaneously, and eight months of Hormuz closure has already proved the system cannot absorb a sustained shutdown without severe cost.

**The resilience camp argues:** the adaptation has been remarkable. Gulf exporters rebuilt flows to near pre-war volumes through pipelines, offshore transfers, and bypass ports; ships rerouted around the Cape after the Red Sea was contested; oil markets, though expensive, have not collapsed. The resilience camp notes that Iran's cable threat is partly deterrence signalling rather than operational intent, and that less than 1% of global bandwidth actually transits Hormuz.

**Both sides accept:** trade and data are concentrated through a handful of chokepoints, redundancy and protection are thin, the cost of disrupting them has fallen dramatically, and the bill for running the world's energy system around a shut Hormuz is now being paid, visibly, through shipping costs and oil prices.

---

### Stress test (the benign case)

The system is more adaptable than it looks. Gulf oil exports rebounded to roughly 16.5 million bpd in September — near pre-war levels — through pipelines, bypass ports, and extensive ship-to-ship transfers, even as Hormuz vessel traffic stayed well below normal; the system is more expensive and increasingly stretched, but it has not collapsed. A durable ceasefire and US blockade-lift could allow a rapid resumption of direct Hormuz transits; prediction markets as of early November put the probability of Hormuz returning to normal traffic by the end of 2026 at around 12%. **\[Forecast\]** The vulnerability remains simultaneity and speed: rerouting works only if one chokepoint fails at a time, and cable cuts give no warning. A compounding event — Hormuz attacks plus Red Sea closure plus a cable cut — would overwhelm the buffers the system has painfully assembled.

---

### Historical parallel

The 2021 grounding of a single container ship in the Suez Canal blocked roughly 12% of global trade for six days and snarled supply chains for months. The 1956 and 1967 Suez closures reordered global shipping for years. The current Hormuz closure — now eight months old and counting — is in a different category: the longest peacetime-equivalent chokepoint disruption since the Suez closures of the Cold War era, and the first to be deliberately compounded by mine-laying, drone attacks on ships, and explicit threats to the digital cables running through the same water. The lesson each of these episodes taught — and which the world keeps relearning — is that chokepoints concentrate efficiency in good times and catastrophe in bad ones.

::: box
**In plain terms**

Almost everything you buy travels by sea, and almost all internet data travels through cables on the ocean floor — and both funnel through a few narrow passages, the most critical of which is the Strait of Hormuz. That passage has been functionally closed for eight months: fewer than 10 ships a day are transiting where 85 once did, tanker attacks set an all-time monthly record in October, and oil is now more than \$27 a barrel higher than it was four months ago. Exporters have partly worked around the blockade through pipelines and long ocean detours, which is why the lights are still on — but at a steeply higher cost that is flowing through to fuel, fertiliser, and goods prices. Now Iran has explicitly threatened the undersea cables running through the same strait, adding a second dimension to the crisis: a cut could disrupt banking, cloud services, and communications across the Gulf region within hours, and repairs take weeks. This is the most active crisis in this report, with no clear diplomatic exit visible.
:::

::: box
**What you can do now**

**Expect fuel and goods prices to stay elevated and volatile.** With Hormuz still shut and tanker attacks at a record, energy-shock inflation will continue to show up at the pump and in shipping-dependent goods; this is not a brief spike.

**Keep a basic resilience kit.** Communications outages — including internet disruption from cable problems — are a real risk in the Gulf region and beyond; the standard advice applies: some cash on hand, a little extra of essential medications and supplies, and offline copies of critical documents.

**Don't over-react in markets to each headline.** Shipping and energy shocks are already substantially priced in; panic-trading geopolitical news rarely helps. A cash buffer and avoiding leverage (see Story 1) are more useful than trying to time the crisis.

**For businesses,** this is the case for mapping your supply chain's chokepoint exposure, building sensible buffer inventory, and checking whether your IT suppliers rely on Gulf-region cable or data-centre infrastructure — just-in-time logistics and cloud dependencies both carry hidden chokepoint risk.
:::

### What to watch

- **Strait of Hormuz transit counts and vessel attacks** (IMF PortWatch daily data; UKMTO incident reports) — any further collapse toward zero, or a genuine diplomatic reopening, is the single most important signal.
- **Cable-threat escalation** — any confirmed cut to the six cable systems threading Hormuz; Iranian military signalling ahead of any resumption of US–Israeli strikes.
- **Oil shipping costs and the Brent–WTI spread** — the cost pass-through to consumers and the spread between the Gulf-exposed benchmark and the US inland benchmark are the clearest market gauges of chokepoint stress.
- **Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb transit volumes and Panama Canal governance** — a second major chokepoint seizing at the same time as Hormuz remains the central stress-test scenario.
